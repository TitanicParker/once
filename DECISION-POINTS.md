# DECISION-POINTS

## Purpose

This register identifies the principal points at which the documentary record shows a clinically material choice, re-evaluation opportunity, treatment substitution, or change in evidential state.

A decision point is not a finding that the decision taken was wrong. It identifies a point at which the available facts, the governing treatment strategy, and later expert questions intersect.

Each entry therefore separates:

- **evidence then available**;
- **decision / management position recorded**;
- **new question created by that decision**;
- **later evidence relevant to whether the position should have been revisited**.

## DP01 — July 2017: initial neurological formulation and treatment strategy

### Evidence then available

Before and at first Neurology assessment, the record contained:

- bilateral foot problems and abnormal gait;
- toe clawing;
- inability to relax;
- increased tone in all four limbs;
- brisk reflexes;
- progressive functional deterioration;
- strikingly abnormal gait;
- dystonic-looking foot-drop-like posture explicitly recorded as “NOT FOOT DROP”;
- bilateral rigidity and gross bradykinesia;
- genuine structural foot pathology;
- unusually young onset; and
- an unresolved relationship between the foot syndrome and Parkinsonism.

**Complete-record anchors:**  
`CLIN-000847–CLIN-000884`  
`CLIN-001084–CLIN-001120`  
`CLIN-001128–CLIN-001155`  
`CLIN-001142–CLIN-001149`

### Recorded management position

Neurology treated the case as young-onset Parkinsonism, referred the structural foot problem to Podiatry, retained the neurological-foot relationship as “Not clear”, and adopted a deliberately cautious strategy toward levodopa/dopamine agonists because of age and anticipated treatment complications.

**Complete-record anchors:**  
`CLIN-001153–CLIN-001180`  
`CLIN-001228–CLIN-001229`

### Decision-point question

Was “Not clear” an adequate endpoint, or should it have functioned as an **active unresolved proposition** requiring a plan to test whether dystonia, tone, gait or loading contributed to the foot syndrome?

### Relevant propositions

P01, P02, P03, P07

## DP02 — July–September 2017: suspected PRKN/PARK2 and specialist threshold

### Evidence then available

The treating neurologist recognised:

- very young onset;
- akinetic rigidity;
- marked symmetry;
- preserved cognition;
- no dominant family history;
- possible recessive Parkinsonism;
- possible PRKN/PARK2;
- possible relevance of genotype to levodopa sensitivity; and
- his own lack of movement-disorders specialist expertise.

**Complete-record anchors:**  
`CLIN-001493–CLIN-001525`  
`CLIN-001546–CLIN-001560`

### Recorded management position

A second opinion was sought from Prof Daniel Healy, including a specific request for advice about genetics and the proposed policy of holding back levodopa.

**Complete-record anchor:** `CLIN-001720–CLIN-001730`

Prof Healy then recorded that the patient “probably has Park II”, noted dramatic treatment responsiveness, described the characteristic low-dose dopamine-replacement / dyskinesia balance, and remained willing to review again.

**Complete-record anchors:**  
`CLIN-001742–CLIN-001749`

### Decision-point question

Did this become merely a one-off second opinion, or had the combination of suspected PARK2, atypical phenotype, treatment sensitivity and high-stakes treatment decisions crossed a threshold for continuing movement-disorders input or later re-referral?

### Relevant propositions

P04, P07, P22

## DP03 — September–October 2017: procyclidine withdrawal created new within-patient evidence

### Evidence then available

Procyclidine produced marked functional improvement.

During planned withdrawal, the patient contemporaneously reported:

- increasing tension;
- restless feet; and
- gait deterioration.

Visual adverse effects improved off treatment.

**Complete-record anchors:**  
`CLIN-001770–CLIN-001790`  
`CLIN-001826–CLIN-001834`

### Recorded management response

Procyclidine was reintroduced at a lower dose and subsequently increased again because the lower dose did not control all symptoms.

**Complete-record anchors:**  
`CLIN-001826–CLIN-001834`

### Decision-point question

Did this withdrawal/rechallenge constitute sufficiently important new evidence that the feet were at least partly medication-state responsive, requiring the original “Not clear” formulation to be updated?

### Relevant propositions

P05, P06, P07

## DP04 — April 2018: focal foot-specific medication-state evidence becomes clinician-documented

### Evidence then available

The treating neurologist recorded that, during the earlier procyclidine withdrawal, the **first thing noticed changing rapidly** was the return of the outside-left-foot/corn phenomenon.

The same note continued to record substantial relief of tension and good gait benefit.

**Complete-record anchors:**  
`CLIN-001890–CLIN-001919`

### Recorded management position

The neurologist also stated that the patient could be “very much better” with levodopa or another dopamine agonist, but continued the prior decision not to use those treatments for the previously stated reasons.

**Complete-record anchors:**  
`CLIN-001943–CLIN-001956`

### Decision-point question

At this stage, should the original dopaminergic-restraint policy have been explicitly re-tested against the new foot-specific medication-state evidence?

A subsidiary question is whether a carefully observed dopaminergic trial could reasonably have been used as a diagnostic/therapeutic probe of the foot syndrome.

### Relevant propositions

P05, P06, P07, P08

## DP05 — 2018–2019: treatment-risk evidence becomes patient-specific

### Evidence then available

Higher-dose Neupro produced:

- chorea/dyskinesia;
- behavioural/impulsivity concerns; and
- a clinically difficult balance between insufficient benefit at lower doses and adverse effects at higher doses.

The clinician described management as an “extremely difficult tight rope”.

**Complete-record anchors:**  
`CLIN-002114–CLIN-002140`  
`CLIN-002146–CLIN-002158`

### Recorded management position

The levodopa-deferral strategy remained active, with a stated preference to leave levodopa for as long as possible.

**Complete-record anchors:**  
`CLIN-002152–CLIN-002158`

### Decision-point question

Did the emergence of **real patient-specific treatment risk** strengthen the case for restraint while simultaneously increasing the need for a more explicit benefit-versus-risk model, including whether lower-limb/foot treatment benefit was being weighed with equal longitudinal force?

### Relevant propositions

P14, P20, P21

## DP06 — September–October 2021: levodopa introduction and procyclidine-to-Sinemet substitution

### Evidence then available

The patient was considered undertreated and Sinemet was introduced for the first time.

Shortly afterwards, because of ocular pressure concerns, Kemadrin/procyclidine was removed and Sinemet increased.

The patient expected the increased Sinemet to “more than compensate” for removal of Kemadrin.

**Complete-record anchors:**  
`CLIN-002330–CLIN-002338`

### Recorded management position

The record focused on general Parkinsonian adequacy, tolerability, dyskinesia and impulse-control concerns while keeping Sinemet relatively low.

**Complete-record anchors:**  
`CLIN-002338–CLIN-002353`

### Decision-point question

Was this substitution ever **validated against the feet as a distinct symptom domain**?

The essential question is:

**known foot medication-state response → procyclidine removed → Sinemet substituted → compensation expected → was foot-specific compensation demonstrated?**

### Relevant propositions

P09, P10, P14, P20

## DP08 — March 2023: severe pain plus contrary medication-withdrawal evidence

### Evidence then available

The March 2023 record documented:

- severe bilateral foot/lower-leg pain;
- pregabalin 600 mg/day;
- debilitating symptoms and poor quality of life; and
- the patient's report that ten days off Parkinson medication had made no perceived difference from a Parkinson's perspective.

**Complete-record anchors:**  
`CLIN-002677–CLIN-002684`

### Recorded management problem

This created evidence pointing in a different direction from the earlier procyclidine withdrawal and later OFF-related observations.

### Decision-point question

How much weight should this uncontrolled ten-day self-withdrawal report carry?

Should it have reduced confidence in a medication-state model, or should it have been treated as competing evidence requiring more symptom-specific testing rather than closure?

### Relevant propositions

P11, P15, P16

## DP09 — Later 2023: Neurology itself classifies pain and shuffling as OFF-related

### Evidence then available

Later in 2023 the record documented:

- severe foot pain;
- reduced mobility and shuffling;
- worsening in the morning and later in the day if Sinemet had not been taken; and
- the clinician's conclusion that the patient appeared undertreated and that the leg pain and shuffling were OFF-related.

**Complete-record anchors:**  
`CLIN-002573–CLIN-002581`

Further later-2023 material described evening protective stance, lack of movement and short steps as sounding like typical OFF Parkinsonian symptoms, with Stalevo being used for “Pep in the Step” and “for pain”.

**Complete-record anchors:**  
`CLIN-002455–CLIN-002470`

### Recorded management response

Dopaminergic treatment was adjusted, but dyskinesia risk remained a constraint.

### Decision-point question

Was this the point at which medication-state sensitivity should have become a **stable longitudinal premise** requiring global re-synthesis of:

- the 2017 “Not clear” formulation;
- dystonic lower-limb findings;
- procyclidine withdrawal evidence;
- 2018 focal foot return;
- 2021 substitution;
- 2022 morning pattern;
- structural Podiatry findings;
- pregabalin response; and
- dyskinesia sensitivity?

### Relevant propositions

P13, P14, P15, P16, P20, P21

### Evidence then available

The 2025 record documented:

- severe morning foot pain;
- “murder in the feet”;
- the first approximately 150 footsteps being torture;
- relief once Parkinson medication hit the system; and
- an approximately 8 PM last Sinemet dose followed by an overnight gap until morning.

**Complete-record anchors:**  
`CLIN-002777–CLIN-002780`  
`CLIN-002794`

### Recorded management response

Neurology introduced Sinemet CR near bedtime to extend dopaminergic coverage overnight.

**Complete-record anchors:**  
`CLIN-002795–CLIN-002800`

### Decision-point question

What does the decision to alter **duration and timing of coverage**, rather than simply total dose, imply about the preceding treatment problem?

Was this a new problem, or the eventual explicit testing of a temporal-coverage hypothesis foreshadowed by the 2022 morning pattern and later-2023 OFF evidence?

### Relevant propositions

P13, P17, P18

## DP12 — 2025: “profound relief” after Sinemet CR

### Evidence then available

The later 2025 complaint response records that Sinemet CR provided “profound relief currently”.

**Complete-record anchors:**  
`CLIN-003263–CLIN-003269`

### Decision-point question

How far may this later natural experiment legitimately revise the evidential weight of earlier observations?

It may bear on:

- the 2022 morning warm-up;
- later-2023 OFF-related pain;
- the 2021 substitution question; and
- earlier procyclidine medication-state evidence.

It does **not** by itself establish:

- one biological pain generator across all years;
- that all historical foot pain was dopaminergic;
- that controlled-release formulation alone caused the improvement; or
- that the same intervention would necessarily have worked years earlier.

### Relevant propositions

P17, P18

## DP13 — Longitudinal management-model decision point

### Evidence across the period

The record contains two accumulating streams:

**Treatment-benefit evidence**
- dramatic procyclidine response;
- deterioration during withdrawal including the feet;
- focal outside-left-foot return;
- later lower-state pain/shuffling;
- Sinemet/Stalevo-associated functional and pain benefit;
- morning relief once Parkinson medication took effect;
- profound Sinemet CR response.

**Treatment-risk evidence**
- visual/ocular procyclidine toxicity;
- dopamine-agonist behavioural effects;
- dopamine-agonist dyskinesia;
- levodopa/Stalevo dyskinesia sensitivity;
- continued concern about dopamine dysregulation/impulse-control effects.

**Complete-record anchors:**  
`CLIN-001770–CLIN-001801`  
`CLIN-001913–CLIN-001919`  
`CLIN-002114–CLIN-002158`  
`CLIN-002573–CLIN-002581`  
`CLIN-002777–CLIN-002800`  
`CLIN-003243–CLIN-003269`

### Decision-point question

Across 2017–2025, was the original strategy of dopaminergic restraint repeatedly re-evaluated as a **revisable management hypothesis**, or did it retain governing force while new evidence was accommodated locally?

A related expert question is whether treatment-risk evidence received more durable longitudinal weight than treatment-benefit evidence relating specifically to the feet/lower limbs.

### Relevant propositions

P19, P20, P21, P22

## Boundary rule

A decision point does **not** establish that:

- a different decision was necessarily required;
- levodopa should necessarily have been started earlier;
- specialist co-management was mandatory at a particular date;
- the feet were wholly neurological;
- the 2021 substitution was inadequate;
- later 2023 was definitively the required point of global re-synthesis; or
- earlier introduction of Sinemet CR would necessarily have produced the later result.

Those are questions for the independent movement-disorders expert.

The purpose of this register is narrower: to identify the points at which the evidential state or therapeutic architecture changed enough that the adequacy of continued assumptions can be tested.
