# How to Read `complete-record.md`

## Purpose

`complete-record.md` is not a single story waiting to be extracted. It is a longitudinal evidential record produced by different people, at different times, for different purposes, under changing clinical circumstances.

It contains patient reports, contemporaneous observations, examination findings, referrals, working diagnoses, treatment plans, medication changes, treatment responses, later interpretations, retrospective explanations, and complaint correspondence.

Because of that, the record can legitimately be read in more than one way.

That fact matters **before any interpretation of the clinical case begins**.

A bare chronological reading may identify one set of facts about what happened. A structured reading can additionally identify facts **about the record itself**: how uncertainty was expressed, how questions were transmitted, how rationales changed, when evidence first appeared, whether observations were representative, and whether later explanations accurately preserve or retrospectively reorganise earlier events.

Those record-level facts do not rewrite the underlying case facts. They can, however, materially change what those case facts are capable of proving.

The purpose of this guide is therefore not to prescribe a conclusion. It is to establish a disciplined way of reading the record so that chronology, hindsight, repetition, retrospective explanation, treatment response and documentary structure do not silently become substitutes for evidence.

The central discipline is:

> **Accept the event. Audit the proposition.**
>
> A document may reliably establish that something was said, observed, prescribed, believed, interpreted or decided. It does not necessarily establish that the interpretation attached to it was independently proven, contemporaneously held, or applicable to an earlier period.

---

# 1. The record has two evidential layers

## 1.1 Facts within the record

These are facts about the clinical case as documented. Examples include:

- a symptom was reported;
- an examination finding was recorded;
- a diagnosis or differential was written;
- a referral was made;
- a medication was prescribed, stopped, increased, reduced or substituted;
- a treatment response was described;
- a patient or clinician expressed a particular view;
- a test produced a result.

These facts tell us **what the record says happened in the case**.

## 1.2 Facts about the record

These are facts about the documentary record as evidence. Examples include:

- an uncertainty appears before a management plan and is not expressly resolved;
- a referral describes a problem but does not transmit the causal question attached to it;
- a later note uses stronger causal language than an earlier note without a clearly documented intermediate resolution;
- a later record repeats an earlier classification without adding new evidence;
- a complaint response retrospectively attributes a purpose to a treatment that is not visible at the time the treatment was initiated;
- a clinic examination occurs during a part of the day the patient later identifies as unusually good;
- a treatment is changed repeatedly while another potentially relevant therapeutic variable remains relatively fixed;
- a later explanation compresses several distinct phases of treatment into a single retrospective strategy.

These facts tell us **how the record itself behaves as evidence**.

## 1.3 Interaction rule

> **Facts about the record may alter the permissible interpretation, evidential independence, temporal reach or causal weight of facts within the record, without changing the fact that those statements, observations or decisions were recorded.**

For example, a 2025 statement about why a medication was “introduced” remains a fact: the clinician said it. But if the contemporaneous record shows that another clinician actually initiated the medication for a different stated purpose, the later statement must be treated as a retrospective account whose historical reach requires testing.

The later statement does not disappear. Its **evidential function changes**.

---

# 2. What multiple legitimate readings do to the case

Before anyone decides what the case means, the existence of multiple legitimate readings changes the status of the record.

The case ceases to be merely a chronology and becomes a **structured evidential object**.

That has several consequences:

- no single chronology is automatically privileged;
- apparent consistency must be tested rather than assumed;
- apparent contradiction may reflect different propositions rather than simple error;
- later knowledge cannot automatically govern the meaning of earlier decisions;
- repetition cannot automatically count as corroboration;
- a clinic observation cannot automatically be assumed to represent the patient's entire medication cycle or day;
- a treatment rationale stated later cannot automatically be treated as the rationale that existed at initiation;
- certainty must be earned by showing that a conclusion survives the different legitimate readings of the record.

The first task is therefore not:

> **What is the story?**

It is:

> **Which propositions are actually supported, by what evidence, at what time, under what observational conditions, and with what documentary limitations?**

# 3. The twelve core reading methods

## 3.1 Bare chronological reading

### What it is

Read the record in date order and identify what happened: presentations, investigations, referrals, diagnoses, treatment changes, symptoms, responses and later correspondence.

### What it identifies

- first appearance of symptoms;
- first objective findings;
- first referral;
- treatment introductions and withdrawals;
- later investigations;
- later explanations.

### Limitation

Chronology alone can make later repetition look like new evidence and can allow hindsight to organise earlier events.

---

## 3.2 Prospective reading

### What it is

Read each entry from the standpoint of the people involved **at that time**.

Ask:

- What was actually known?
- What was suspected?
- What remained unresolved?
- What evidence existed before the next decision?
- What later evidence did not yet exist?

### What it identifies

The real evidential state before each decision.

This is essential where later knowledge makes an earlier uncertainty appear more settled than it actually was.

### Control

> **Later knowledge must not be back-projected into an earlier decision.**

---

## 3.3 Retrospective reading

### What it is

Read earlier events in light of later evidence: later treatment responses, later classifications, later genetics, later symptom patterns and later complaint explanations.

### What it identifies

Whether later evidence:

- supports an earlier hypothesis;
- weakens it;
- clarifies an ambiguity;
- exposes that an earlier uncertainty persisted;
- or creates an apparently coherent story that was not contemporaneously available.

### Limitation

> **Retrospective coherence is not contemporaneous knowledge.**

---

## 3.4 Document-architecture reading

### What it is

Examine how the documents themselves are constructed.

Relevant features include:

- headings;
- diagnosis fields;
- narrative order;
- repetition;
- addenda;
- structured discharge fields;
- compressed summaries;
- later material inserted beside earlier reasoning.

### What it identifies

How document structure can increase or decrease the apparent certainty of a proposition.

A categorical diagnosis field may coexist with a much more qualified narrative. A summary can operationally compress uncertainty without resolving it.

### Control

> **Physical placement in a document does not necessarily establish chronology of thought.**

---

## 3.5 Management-plan reading

### What it is

Trace the governing management strategy independently of the diagnostic narrative.

Ask:

- What became an explicit policy?
- What risks justified it?
- What information was expected to change it?
- What uncertainties were treated as non-determinative?
- When was the policy reconsidered?

### What it identifies

The architecture by which clinical beliefs actually governed care.

A diagnosis may remain stable while the rationale for treatment changes. Conversely, an unresolved causal question may remain outside the main management architecture for years.

---

## 3.6 Referral-pathway reading

### What it is

Treat every referral as a transfer of a clinical task.

Ask:

1. What facts were transmitted?
2. What question was asked?
3. Was the purpose treatment, rehabilitation, investigation, second opinion or diagnostic clarification?
4. What was the receiving service expected to resolve?
5. What can legitimately be inferred from its response?

### What it identifies

The difference between referral to **manage a manifestation** and referral to **resolve a causal question**.

### Referral-purpose control

> **Do not read the existence of a referral as proof that the uncertainty associated with the referral was actually referred.**

And:

> **Evidence that a service treated manifestations within its remit does not establish that it considered, excluded or resolved causal questions that were not clearly posed to it.**

---

## 3.7 Causal-proposition reading

### What it is

Break broad labels such as “foot pain,” “the feet,” or “Parkinson's symptoms” into separate propositions.

Possible levels include:

- structural pathology;
- focal painful lesion;
- dystonic or Parkinsonian posture;
- abnormal mechanical loading;
- peripheral neuropathic process;
- medication-state modulation;
- amplification of pain experience;
- functional consequence.

### What it identifies

Which specific proposition each item of evidence actually addresses.

### Scope control

> **Evidence resolving one proposition must not silently resolve adjacent propositions.**

A Parkinsonian movement does not prove the cause of every painful lesion. A structural lesion does not prove that neurological state cannot alter the pain arising at that site.

---

## 3.8 Evidential-development reading

### What it is

Classify each later statement according to what it actually adds.

Useful categories are:

### A. New observation

A new examination finding, symptom behaviour, treatment withdrawal effect, test result or ON/OFF observation.

### B. New interpretation of new evidence

A clinician newly classifies a phenomenon after observing something new.

### C. Inherited framing

A later record repeats or presupposes an earlier classification without adding new evidence.

### D. Retrospective reconstruction

A later clinician explains what was supposedly intended, understood or known earlier.

### What it identifies

Whether the evidential state actually changed or whether only the language changed.

### Inheritance control

> **Repeated terminology is not independent corroboration unless new evidence accompanies it.**

---

## 3.9 Patient–clinician perspective reading

### What it is

Preserve distinctions between:

- what the patient experienced;
- what the patient believed caused it;
- what a clinician observed;
- what the clinician inferred;
- what the clinician later explained.

### What it identifies

Where experience and interpretation converge, diverge or change over time.

A patient's belief is not objective proof. A clinician's interpretation is not automatically objective proof either. Both are evidence of perspective and may become important in understanding later disagreement.

---

## 3.10 Uncertainty-and-closure reading

### What it is

Track each important question from the moment it appears.

Ask whether it is:

- explicitly resolved;
- narrowed;
- referred elsewhere;
- left open;
- replaced by a working assumption;
- or allowed to become operationally irrelevant without documented causal closure.

### What it identifies

The fate of uncertainty.

A useful distinction is:

- **operational closure** — enough certainty to make a management decision;
- **causal closure** — enough evidence to determine why a manifestation occurred.

A record may legitimately reach the first without reaching the second.

---

## 3.11 Treatment-response reading

### What it is

Treat medication introductions, withdrawals, substitutions, timing changes and formulation changes as potential natural experiments.

Ask:

- What changed immediately before the response?
- Which symptom changed?
- Which symptom did not?
- Was the response contemporaneous or retrospective?
- Was there a confounder?
- Was the change in pain, movement, posture, function or more than one domain?

### What it identifies

Association, state dependence, dissociation and potential therapeutic signals.

### Control

> **Treatment response does not prove mechanism by itself.**

It may establish that a symptom is neurologically modifiable without proving that the underlying structural lesion was neurologically generated.

---

## 3.12 Complaint-response reading

### What it is

Treat later complaint responses as retrospective explanatory documents rather than privileged summaries of the earlier record.

Ask:

- What does the response say was known earlier?
- When is that understanding first visible contemporaneously?
- Does it distinguish original treatment rationale from later treatment function?
- Does it preserve earlier uncertainty?
- Does it compress several phases into one retrospective strategy?

### What it identifies

The relationship between the historical record and the clinician's later reconstruction of it.

### Core control

> **The clinician writing in 2025 is not a narrator standing outside the record. The 2025 clinician is another participant producing another document at another time.**

A retrospective explanation is evidence of the later explanation. Its temporal reach must be independently tested.

---

# 4. Five cross-cutting controls

The twelve readings above should now be applied through five additional controls. These controls arose from close examination of the medication and observation history and should apply throughout the record.

---

## 4.1 Contemporaneous rationale versus later-attributed rationale

For every important treatment or decision, separate four questions:

1. **Who initiated it?**
2. **Why was it initiated at that time?**
3. **Why was it later continued, changed or stopped?**
4. **What purpose was later attributed to it retrospectively?**

These are different propositions.

A treatment can acquire a new function within management after it has already been initiated. That later function does not necessarily establish the original prescribing rationale.

### Example: pregabalin/Lyrica

The contemporaneous 2023 record identifies pregabalin as a GP-originated treatment for severe burning/neuropathic-type pain. Later Neurology records show it being continued when the patient reported substantial benefit. A 2025 complaint response later describes pregabalin as having been introduced partly to spare further Sinemet increase.

Those statements must not be collapsed into one proposition.

The record should distinguish:

- **original initiation rationale**;
- **later continuation rationale**;
- **later management function**;
- **retrospective description of the whole history**.

### Control

> **A later rationale may accurately describe what a treatment eventually did within the management strategy without proving that the same rationale caused its original initiation.**

---

## 4.2 Medication-state trace

Do not read medication history merely as a list of drug names and doses.

Trace symptom behaviour against neurological medication state:

- withdrawal;
- onset of effect;
- wearing off;
- replacement of one class with another;
- timing of doses;
- overnight gaps;
- formulation changes;
- response to reintroduction.

### Why this matters

The foot/MTPJ history contains an early natural experiment involving procyclidine withdrawal. The record describes deterioration in walking and restless feet during withdrawal, and later specifically records rapid return of the outside-left-foot/corn phenomenon while off procyclidine.

That does not prove that the structural corn was neurologically generated.

It does establish something narrower and important:

> **the symptom complex at that anatomical site was capable of changing with neurological medication state.**

Once such a relationship is established, later foot-pain entries should be read with that earlier state sensitivity still visible rather than allowing it to disappear behind later diagnostic labels.

### Control

> **Do not allow a later label such as “neuropathic pain” to erase an earlier demonstrated medication-state relationship. Likewise, do not allow medication-state responsiveness to erase documented structural pathology.**

---

## 4.3 Therapeutic-variable disaggregation

Drug treatment is not a single variable.

Always distinguish:

- **drug class**;
- **dose**;
- **formulation**;
- **timing**;
- **frequency**;
- **duration of coverage**;
- **relationship to meals or sleep where relevant**.

### Why this matters

A decision not to increase the **amount** of levodopa because of dyskinesia risk is not identical to a decision about whether the **timing or formulation** of levodopa is appropriate for a predictable period of poor symptom control.

Similarly, an overnight controlled-release formulation can test a different therapeutic proposition from merely increasing daytime immediate-release doses.

### Control

> **Dose ≠ formulation ≠ timing ≠ duration of coverage.**

A record that repeatedly discusses dose escalation may still leave a formulation or coverage question substantially unexplored.

---

## 4.4 Observation-window and representativeness reading

A clinic examination is a sample of the patient's state, not the whole state.

For every important observation ask:

- What time of day was the patient seen?
- When had medication last been taken?
- Was the patient ON, OFF or transitioning?
- Was this normally a good or bad part of the day?
- Did the patient say the observation or video was representative?
- Were worse states occurring outside the clinic window?

### Why this matters

The record eventually states explicitly that morning Neurology clinic was “not ideal” because morning was the patient's best time of day. Earlier, in 2023, the patient was already explaining that an evening video being considered was not representative of his usual evening impairment.

These are facts **about the observational conditions of the record**.

A relatively good morning examination may be entirely accurate while still underrepresenting the severity or character of symptoms occurring later.

### Control

> **An accurate observation is not necessarily a representative observation.**

And:

> **The evidential weight of a clinic examination depends partly on where it falls within the patient's daily and medication-state cycle.**

---

## 4.5 Non-monotonic treatment-response reading

Do not compress variable treatment response into a simple statement such as “the drug worked” or “the drug failed.”

Instead, preserve the treatment state at each point.

### Example: pregabalin/Lyrica

The record contains materially different states:

- severe debilitating pain while taking 600 mg/day;
- later reports of substantial pain benefit while still taking 600 mg/day;
- later residual left-sided pain and persistent toe phenomena despite reported improvement;
- substantial self-reduction by 2025;
- recurrence of severe pain;
- retrospective acknowledgement that even at maximum dose pain had not been eliminated.

These observations do not form a simple monotonic dose-response curve.

### What that shows

The contradiction or variability is itself evidentially important.

The reader should not average it into a cleaner narrative.

### Control

> **When treatment response changes over time, preserve the variation before attempting to explain it.**

# 5. A worked example of why these controls matter

The foot-pain history illustrates how the same entries produce different evidential conclusions depending on how they are read.

A bare reading may produce:

> structural foot problems → neuropathic-type pain → pregabalin treatment → later levodopa-related pain improvement.

A more disciplined record-level reading reveals several overlapping tracks:

1. **Structural pathology is repeatedly documented and treated.**
2. **The relationship between that structural/painful foot syndrome and the neurological disorder is expressly uncertain in 2017.**
3. **Withdrawal of procyclidine produces early evidence that the foot/MTPJ symptom complex is neurologically medication-state sensitive.**
4. **Procyclidine is later removed and replaced by levodopa.**
5. **Morning-patterned foot symptoms are visible after levodopa introduction.**
6. **Pregabalin is escalated from a very low dose to maximum dose during a severe pain crisis, yet pain behaviour is not consistently dose-dependent.**
7. **Later entries increasingly document OFF-related worsening and relief when Parkinson medication reaches the system.**
8. **Ordinary levodopa escalation is constrained by dyskinesia risk.**
9. **A later controlled-release formulation changes the temporal coverage and is followed by major reported relief.**
10. **The later complaint response retrospectively presents the treatment history as more integrated than the contemporaneous initiation record alone demonstrates.**

None of those propositions requires the reader to decide that the pain was purely structural, purely neuropathic or purely Parkinsonian.

Instead, the record supports a more careful distinction between:

- pain generator;
- mechanical consequence;
- motor/dystonic contribution;
- medication-state modulation;
- pain amplification;
- therapeutic response.

# 6. Special caution: the July 2017 discharge summary

The July 2017 discharge summary should be read at several levels.

A bare reading sees a prominent Parkinson's diagnosis and a detailed treatment plan.

A prospective and architectural reading shows more complexity:

- syndrome-level Parkinsonism is sufficiently established to drive management;
- the exact disease form remains under consideration;
- dystonic-looking foot posture is described;
- structural foot abnormalities are present;
- the relationship between corns/calluses/burning and the neurological syndrome is expressly described as `Not clear`;
- the Plan proceeds without resolving every causal proposition.

That is not inherently improper. Clinical management frequently proceeds under uncertainty.

But it establishes the evidential baseline from which later claims must be judged.

The document therefore demonstrates the distinction between:

> **operational management closure**

and

> **causal non-closure**.

Podiatry therefore establishes what it observed and treated. It should not automatically be read as having resolved a broader neurological-causal question that was not clearly transmitted.

# 8. Special caution: medication-state evidence at the foot/MTPJ

The early procyclidine withdrawal episode should be treated as an important evidential junction.

The safe proposition is not:

> the corn was caused by Parkinsonism.

The safe proposition is:

> **the symptom complex involving the outside-left-foot/MTPJ region was shown early in the record to be neurologically modifiable.**

That fact should remain available when reading later entries concerning:

- recurrent pain;
- morning difficulty;
- OFF states;
- toe posturing;
- levodopa timing;
- Stalevo response;
- eventual controlled-release treatment.

It does not settle the generator. It does constrain later interpretation.

# 9. Special caution: the pregabalin/Lyrica history

Pregabalin should be read prospectively rather than through the final retrospective summary.

The reader should distinguish:

- GP initiation for severe burning/neuropathic-type pain;
- escalation through multiple prescribers;
- severe pain despite maximum-dose treatment in early 2023;
- later reported substantial benefit;
- continued treatment by Neurology;
- later self-reduction;
- recurrence of severe pain;
- later retrospective characterisation of pregabalin as partly levodopa-sparing.

The final retrospective rationale may accurately describe how pregabalin later functioned in management.

It does not, by itself, establish that levodopa-sparing was the reason the drug was originally introduced.

This distinction should be applied to all important treatments in the record.

# 10. Special caution: clinic timing and representativeness

The record itself raises the question of whether clinic observation systematically captured the patient's best state.

The reader should therefore avoid treating a morning examination as a neutral sample of the whole day.

The relevant questions are:

- Was morning ordinarily the patient's best state?
- Were disabling symptoms primarily occurring later?
- Was the patient observed before or after medication onset?
- Were videos or descriptions offered because clinic observation failed to reproduce the worst state?
- Did the record adapt its assessment strategy once this limitation became apparent?

This is not an allegation that examinations were inaccurate.

It is a question of **sampling**.

> **An examination can be accurate and still systematically under-represent the clinically important state.**

---

# 11. Special caution: later genetic confirmation

Later PRKN/Parkin evidence is highly relevant to the neurological diagnosis.

It must not be back-projected as historical knowledge.

Nor should confirmation of PRKN Parkinsonism be silently extended into propositions the genetic result does not itself establish, such as the origin of every painful foot lesion or structural abnormality.

---

# 12. Special caution: later treatment success

Later treatment response can materially change the evidential state.

It can show that a symptom is modulated by medication state and can strengthen a therapeutic hypothesis.

It does not automatically prove:

- the original pain generator;
- that the same intervention would have worked identically years earlier;
- that earlier treatment was necessarily unreasonable;
- that an earlier intervention would have produced the same benefit without unacceptable adverse effects.

Those are separate clinical and counterfactual questions.

But later treatment success can legitimately prompt a documentary question:

> **Was the proposition eventually demonstrated by the treatment response already sufficiently visible earlier that it should have been tested sooner?**

That is a question for decision-point and expert analysis, not an answer supplied by hindsight alone.

---

# 13. Special caution: later explanations of earlier management

Retrospective clinician explanations are evidence of what the clinician later says was intended, understood or balanced.

They must be tested against contemporaneous records.

For every retrospective claim ask:

- When does this rationale first appear?
- Is it contemporaneous with the decision being described?
- Was the author actually responsible for the original decision?
- Does the earlier record give a different stated purpose?
- Could the later statement reasonably be describing a function the treatment acquired later rather than its original purpose?

### Core principle

> **Do not permit a later clinical summary to become the authoritative narrator of the earlier record merely because it was written by the treating clinician.**

---

# 14. The four histories running through the record

Taken together, the reading methods reveal at least four overlapping histories.

## 14.1 The history of what happened

Symptoms, observations, investigations, referrals, treatments, responses and outcomes.

## 14.2 The history of what was believed

The changing interpretations attached to those events.

## 14.3 The history of how belief governed management

Which propositions actually drove decisions, which risks constrained treatment, and which uncertainties were allowed to remain.

## 14.4 The history of the questions themselves

When an uncertainty arose, whether it was converted into a diagnostic task, whether new evidence altered it, whether it was expressly resolved, and whether it instead persisted outside the main decision architecture.

A fifth useful history can be added:

## 14.5 The history of how the history was later told

How later notes and complaint responses reconstructed earlier intent, knowledge and treatment purpose.

None of these histories should be mistaken for the whole case.

# 15. A practical method for reading every material entry

For each important entry, ask the following in order.

1. **Event** — What happened or was recorded?
2. **Source** — Patient report, clinician observation, test, referral, treatment response, later explanation?
3. **Time** — What was known at that precise point?
4. **Observation window** — At what time of day and medication state was the patient observed?
5. **Proposition** — What does the source say the event means?
6. **Status** — Observation, working diagnosis, hypothesis, explicit uncertainty, management decision or retrospective reconstruction?
7. **Contemporaneous rationale** — Why was the decision made then?
8. **Later-attributed rationale** — Was a different purpose later assigned to it?
9. **New evidence** — What genuinely changed the evidential state?
10. **Inherited framing** — What language was merely carried forward?
11. **Therapeutic variable** — Was the relevant change in drug, dose, formulation, timing or duration of coverage?
12. **Scope** — Exactly which causal proposition does the evidence address?
13. **Decision** — What management followed?
14. **Unresolved question** — What remained open?
15. **Re-synthesis** — Did later evidence prompt an explicit reconstruction of the earlier formulation?

This sequence is designed to prevent several common shortcuts:

> diagnosis appears repeatedly → therefore every later manifestation was independently shown to belong to that diagnosis;

> structural pathology appears repeatedly → therefore neurological medication-state effects are irrelevant;

> a later clinician states a rationale → therefore that was the original rationale;

> a drug helped at one point → therefore it consistently controlled the symptom;

> clinic examination looked relatively good → therefore the patient's overall daily state was relatively good.

Each of those may be false even when every individual entry is accurately documented.

# 16. Neutral interpretive principles

The following principles should govern use of `complete-record.md`:

- **Do not infer negligence, dishonesty, motive or absence of thought merely from documentary silence.**
- **Do not convert a recorded clinical opinion into objective fact.**
- **Do not convert patient belief into objective fact.**
- **Do not treat treatment response as proof of mechanism.**
- **Do not assume structural and neurological explanations are mutually exclusive.**
- **Do not confuse pain generation with pain modulation or amplification.**
- **Do not use later knowledge as if it existed earlier.**
- **Do not count repeated terminology as independent corroboration unless new evidence accompanies it.**
- **Do not infer that a referred service answered a question that was never clearly referred to it.**
- **Do not confuse management under uncertainty with resolution of uncertainty.**
- **Do not treat later-attributed rationale as contemporaneous rationale without documentary support.**
- **Do not treat drug dose, formulation, timing and coverage as the same therapeutic variable.**
- **Do not assume an accurate clinic observation is representative of the patient's whole day.**
- **Do not compress variable treatment response into a single statement of success or failure.**
- **Do not allow our own synthesis to become cleaner than the source record.**

---

# 17. The central reading questions

The record should be approached repeatedly with two related questions.

First:

> **What was known here, what remained uncertain, what genuinely new evidence entered, what decision followed, and did that evidence require the earlier formulation or management architecture to be reconsidered?**

Second:

> **Is the proposition I am relying on a fact about the clinical case, or a fact about how the record later describes the clinical case?**

Those questions should be asked at every material decision point rather than answered once for the whole record.

---

# 18. Closing principle

`complete-record.md` supports more than one reasonable reading because it contains genuine structural pathology, a well-established neurological disorder, changing medication-state evidence, variable treatment response, differing patient and clinician perspectives, explicit uncertainty, observational limitations, later reinterpretation and a management strategy that evolved over years.

The existence of multiple legitimate readings is not itself a defect in the record.

It means that interpretation requires stricter controls.

A literal reading identifies the contents of the record. A structured reading also identifies the properties of the record itself.

Those properties do not rewrite the clinical facts.

They determine what those facts are capable of proving.

The purpose of this guide is therefore not to tell the reader **which conclusion to adopt**.

It is to make it difficult for any reader — patient, clinician, reviewer, expert, lawyer, investigator or analyst — to unconsciously turn a complex longitudinal evidential record into a cleaner, more certain or more retrospective story than the documents actually support.
