# How to Read `complete-record.md`

## Purpose

`complete-record.md` is not best understood as a simple chronology or as a single clinical narrative. It contains different kinds of material produced at different times for different purposes: contemporaneous observations, patient reports, examination findings, referrals, working diagnoses, treatment plans, treatment responses, later interpretations, retrospective explanations, and complaint correspondence.

A reader can therefore reach very different impressions depending on how the record is approached.

This companion document does **not** prescribe a conclusion. Its purpose is to make those different modes of reading explicit, preserve uncertainty where the record preserves uncertainty, and prevent later knowledge or repeated terminology from silently replacing the evidential state that existed at an earlier date.

The central discipline is:

> **Accept the event. Audit the proposition.**
>
> A later document may reliably establish that something was said, observed, prescribed, believed, or interpreted. It does not necessarily establish that the interpretation attached to it was independently proven.

---

# 1. Bare chronological reading

## What it is

Read the record in date order and identify what happened: presentations, investigations, referrals, diagnoses, treatment changes, symptoms, responses, and later correspondence.

## What it shows

This establishes the basic sequence of events without initially deciding what those events mean.

It is useful for identifying:

- when symptoms first enter the record;
- when objective findings first appear;
- when referrals occur;
- when treatment is introduced, withdrawn, increased, or reduced;
- when new investigations are performed;
- when later explanations are written.

## Limitation

Chronology alone can conceal the difference between fact and interpretation. A later note may repeat an earlier diagnosis or causal framing and appear to add evidence when it is only carrying a label forward.

---

# 2. Prospective reading

## What it is

Read each entry from the standpoint of the people involved **at that time**.

Ask:

- What was actually known?
- What was suspected?
- What remained uncertain?
- What evidence was available before the next decision?
- What later information was not yet available?

## What it shows

This reveals the true evidential state before each management decision.

For example, the July 2017 discharge record can be read prospectively as establishing a Parkinsonian syndrome sufficiently for management while still expressly leaving the relationship between the neurological syndrome and the corns/calluses/burning/foot-pain strand unresolved.

## Control

Later genetic confirmation, later treatment response, or later retrospective explanation must not be back-projected as if it were known in 2017.

---

# 3. Retrospective reading

## What it is

Read the earlier record with the benefit of later events: later treatment response, later neurological classifications, later pain behaviour, later genetics, and later complaint responses.

## What it shows

Retrospective reading can identify whether later evidence:

- supports an earlier hypothesis;
- weakens an earlier hypothesis;
- clarifies an earlier ambiguity;
- exposes that an earlier uncertainty remained unresolved for longer than may be apparent from later summaries.

## Limitation

Retrospective coherence is not the same as contemporaneous knowledge.

A later event can make an earlier hypothesis look prescient without proving that the later-established proposition was already known or sufficiently supported at the earlier date.

---

# 4. Document-architecture reading

## What it is

Examine how the documents themselves are constructed.

Relevant features include:

- headings;
- diagnosis fields;
- the order in which findings and conclusions appear;
- repetition;
- addenda;
- structured discharge fields;
- narrative qualification;
- compression of complex reasoning into short labels.

## What it shows

Document structure can make a proposition appear more settled than the underlying narrative actually makes it.

A categorical diagnosis field may appear before a more qualified differential. A later test may be inserted into a finalised document even though the management narrative was originally formed earlier. A final structured summary may compress a complicated and partly unresolved narrative into a few operational labels.

## Control

Physical placement in a document does not necessarily prove chronology of clinical thought.

---

# 5. Management-plan reading

## What it is

Trace the governing management strategy independently of the diagnostic narrative.

Ask:

- What became an explicit policy?
- What risks justified that policy?
- What information was expected to change it?
- What uncertainties were treated as non-determinative of management?
- When was the policy revisited?

## What it shows

This distinguishes a working diagnosis from the management architecture built around it.

In this record, levodopa/dopamine-replacement restraint became an explicit longitudinal policy in the context of very young onset and concern about dyskinesia and behavioural complications. Genetic subtype was expressly treated as unlikely to alter immediate management. The foot-pain relationship, by contrast, was left unresolved and managed through a separate Podiatry pathway.

The question is therefore not merely what diagnosis existed, but how the management strategy behaved as later evidence accumulated.

---

# 6. Referral-pathway reading

## What it is

Treat each referral as a transfer of a clinical task.

For every referral ask:

1. What facts were transmitted?
2. What question was asked?
3. Was the referral for treatment, rehabilitation, investigation, second opinion, or diagnostic clarification?
4. What was the receiving service expected to resolve?
5. What can legitimately be inferred from the receiving service's response?

## What it shows

This distinguishes a referral to **manage a problem** from a referral to **answer a question about a problem**.

That distinction is important in the Podiatry pathway.

The surviving Podiatry referral material is descriptively informative: it records foot pain, callus/corns, toe deformity, gait abnormality and other structural/mechanical features. But the broader causal question identified by Neurology — the relationship between structural foot pathology, burning/pain, neurological posture and possible neuropathy — is not clearly translated into a question for Podiatry to resolve.

By comparison, other referrals in the record often explicitly state the uncertainty to be addressed: possible Parkinsonism or other diagnosis, appropriate genetic testing, whether an external specialist would alter the levodopa plan, or the need for urgent review of severe burning foot pain.

## Referral-purpose control

> **A referral should be interpreted according to the question actually transmitted to the receiving service. Evidence that a service treated manifestations within its remit does not establish that it considered, excluded or resolved causal questions that were not clearly posed to it.**

---

# 7. Causal-proposition reading

## What it is

Do not treat broad labels such as “the feet,” “foot pain,” or “Parkinson's symptoms” as single causal propositions.

Separate the possible levels:

- local structural or peripheral pain generators;
- motor or dystonic posture;
- mechanical loading or consequences of abnormal posture;
- Parkinsonian medication-state modulation;
- amplification of pain experience;
- functional consequence.

## What it shows

Different pieces of evidence may establish different levels without establishing the entire chain.

For example:

- objective callus or deformity can establish structural pathology;
- a movement can be classified as Parkinsonian;
- pain can improve with pregabalin;
- toe curling can persist after pain improves;
- pain can improve after dopaminergic medication reaches the system.

Those facts can coexist without proving that one single mechanism generated every manifestation.

## Scope control

> **Evidence resolving one proposition must not silently be treated as resolving adjacent propositions.**

Confirmation that a movement is Parkinsonian does not by itself determine the origin of all foot pain. Confirmation of PRKN Parkinsonism does not by itself determine the origin of longstanding focal structural lesions.

---

# 8. Evidential-development reading

## What it is

For each later entry, decide whether it contributes genuinely new evidence or merely continues an earlier classification.

Useful categories are:

### A. New observation

A new examination finding, objective structural finding, new symptom behaviour, treatment withdrawal effect, test result, or temporal ON/OFF observation.

### B. New interpretation of new evidence

A clinician newly classifies an observed phenomenon, for example as Parkinsonian, dystonic, neuropathic, or OFF-related.

### C. Inherited framing

A later record repeats or presupposes an earlier diagnosis or causal description without adding new supporting evidence.

### D. Retrospective reconstruction

A later clinician explains what was supposedly understood or intended earlier.

## What it shows

This prevents repetition from being mistaken for corroboration.

## Inheritance control

> **A later record does not acquire independent causal force merely because it adopts terminology created by an earlier record in which the relevant causal relationship remained unresolved.**

The same principle applies in both directions: repeated structural terminology does not prove all later pain was mechanical, and repeated Parkinsonian terminology does not prove all foot pain was neurologically generated.

---

# 9. Patient–clinician perspective reading

## What it is

Preserve the distinction between:

- what the patient experienced;
- what the patient believed caused it;
- what a clinician observed;
- what a clinician inferred;
- what a clinician later retrospectively explained.

## What it shows

Patient experience and clinician interpretation may converge, diverge, or change over time.

The patient may distinguish pain from tension while a clinician classifies a movement as Parkinsonian. The patient may believe a pain syndrome is separate while the clinician regards its severity as modulated by Parkinsonian OFF state. Neither category should silently replace the other.

This reading is particularly important where later complaint correspondence describes a perceived “misalignment” between patient and clinician.

---

# 10. Uncertainty-and-closure reading

## What it is

Track each important question from the moment it appears.

Ask whether it is:

- explicitly resolved;
- narrowed;
- left open;
- referred elsewhere;
- replaced by a working assumption;
- or allowed to become operationally irrelevant without documented causal closure.

## What it shows

Clinical management can legitimately proceed under uncertainty. Therefore the existence of an unresolved question does not itself establish that management was inappropriate.

The documentary question is narrower:

> **What happened to the uncertainty?**

A useful distinction is between:

- **operational closure** — enough certainty to make a management decision; and
- **causal closure** — enough evidence to determine why a manifestation occurred.

A record can reach the first without reaching the second.

---

# 11. Treatment-response reading

## What it is

Treat medication introductions, withdrawals, substitutions, dose changes, and timing changes as potential natural experiments.

Ask:

- What changed before and after treatment?
- Which symptom changed?
- Which symptom did not?
- Was the observation contemporaneous or retrospective?
- Were there confounders?
- Did benefit concern movement, pain, function, or more than one domain?

## What it shows

Treatment response can demonstrate association, state dependence, or dissociation.

It does **not**, by itself, establish mechanism.

For example, pain relief after dopaminergic treatment may support Parkinsonian modulation of pain experience without proving that Parkinsonism directly generated the underlying painful lesion.

Similarly, improvement with pregabalin may demonstrate an analgesic effect without proving a particular neuropathic cause.

---

# 12. Complaint-response reading

## What it is

Treat later complaint responses as retrospective explanatory documents and compare them with the contemporaneous record.

Ask:

- What does the response say was known earlier?
- When is that understanding first visible contemporaneously?
- Does the response accurately distinguish original prescribing rationale from later management rationale?
- Does it preserve earlier uncertainty, or compress it?
- Does it acknowledge the patient's experience separately from causal interpretation?

## What it shows

Complaint responses can provide valuable evidence about a clinician's later understanding of the case, but they cannot by themselves establish the exact historical date at which that understanding first arose.

Statements such as “we have understood this for quite some time” therefore need to be temporally located against the earlier record.

---

# What the twelve readings reveal together

Taken together, these methods reveal at least four overlapping histories within `complete-record.md`.

## 1. The history of what happened

Symptoms, examinations, investigations, referrals, treatments, responses and outcomes.

## 2. The history of what was believed

The changing diagnostic and causal interpretations attached to those events.

## 3. The history of how belief governed management

Which propositions were considered sufficiently established to drive decisions, which risks dominated treatment strategy, and which uncertainties were allowed to remain while care proceeded.

## 4. The history of the questions themselves

When an uncertainty first arose, whether it was converted into a referral question, whether new evidence altered it, whether it was expressly resolved, and whether it instead became absorbed into an operational pathway without causal closure.

No one of these histories should be mistaken for the whole case.

---

# A practical method for reading each material entry

For any important entry, use the following sequence:

1. **Event** — What actually happened or was recorded?
2. **Source** — Patient report, clinician observation, test, referral, treatment response, retrospective explanation?
3. **Proposition** — What does the source say the event means?
4. **Status** — Fact, observation, working diagnosis, hypothesis, interpretation, explicit uncertainty, management decision, or retrospective reconstruction?
5. **New evidence?** — Does this add something genuinely new?
6. **Inherited framing?** — Is it merely repeating an earlier categorisation?
7. **Scope** — Exactly which proposition does the evidence support?
8. **Decision** — What management followed?
9. **Unresolved question** — What remained open afterward?
10. **Later effect** — Was that open question later revisited, reclassified, or merely carried forward?

This prevents the common documentary shortcut:

> diagnosis appears repeatedly → therefore every later manifestation was independently shown to belong to that diagnosis.

It also prevents the opposite shortcut:

> structural pathology appears repeatedly → therefore later medication-state effects cannot be clinically relevant.

Both may be false reductions of a mixed record.

# Special caution: the July 2017 discharge summary

The July 2017 discharge summary should be read in more than one way.

A bare reading sees a prominent Parkinson's diagnosis and a detailed treatment plan.

A closer architectural reading shows that the narrative is more qualified: it distinguishes syndrome-level Parkinsonism from uncertainty about disease form, discusses possible genetic causes, records dystonic-looking gait and structural foot abnormalities, and expressly says the relationship between corns/calluses/burning and the neurological syndrome is “Not clear.”

The Plan begins without resolving every causal question.

That is not inherently improper. Medicine frequently requires action under uncertainty.

But it establishes the baseline evidential state from which later entries must be judged.

The document therefore demonstrates a distinction between:

> **operational management closure**

and

> **causal non-closure**.

# Special caution: the Podiatry pathway

Podiatry should neither be dismissed nor over-read.

The pathway provides important objective evidence of local structural and mechanical pathology, including recurrent callus/HD, toe deformity/clawing, pressure sites, focal pain, pronation, offloading, toe props, insoles and repeated debridement.

But the referral material does not clearly ask Podiatry to adjudicate the full relationship between:

- structural pathology;
- dystonic or Parkinsonian posturing;
- abnormal loading;
- neuropathic possibilities;
- medication-state effects;
- pain amplification.

Therefore Podiatry can support propositions about what it observed and treated without necessarily resolving the wider neurological-causal question.

---

# Special caution: later genetic confirmation

The later PRKN/Parkin result is highly relevant to the neurological diagnosis.

It should not be back-projected as historical knowledge.

Nor should confirmation that Parkinsonism/dystonia is due to PRKN mutations be silently extended into propositions that the genetic result does not itself establish, such as the historical origin of every painful foot lesion or structural abnormality.

---

# Special caution: later treatment success

A later treatment response can alter the evidential state substantially.

It can show that a symptom is modulated by medication state, and a targeted intervention can strengthen that inference.

It does not automatically prove:

- the original generator of the symptom;
- that the same treatment would have produced the same result years earlier;
- that earlier uncertainty was unreasonable;
- or that earlier treatment would necessarily have been clinically preferable.

Those are separate questions and may require expert evidence.

---

# Special caution: later explanations of earlier management

Retrospective clinician explanations are important evidence of what the clinician later says was intended, understood, or balanced.

They should be tested against contemporaneous documentation.

A later rationale may accurately describe how a medication eventually functioned in the management strategy without proving that the same rationale caused the original prescribing decision.

Similarly, a later statement that a phenomenon had been understood “for some time” should prompt the reader to identify the earliest contemporaneous entry that actually demonstrates that understanding.

---

# Neutral interpretive principles

The following principles should govern use of the record:

- **Do not infer negligence, dishonesty, motive, or absence of thought merely from documentary silence.**
- **Do not convert a recorded opinion into objective fact.**
- **Do not convert patient belief into objective fact.**
- **Do not treat treatment response as proof of mechanism.**
- **Do not assume structural and neurological explanations are mutually exclusive.**
- **Do not confuse pain generation with pain modulation or amplification.**
- **Do not use later knowledge as if it existed earlier.**
- **Do not count repeated terminology as independent corroboration unless new evidence accompanies it.**
- **Do not infer that a referred service answered a question that was never clearly referred to it.**
- **Do distinguish management under uncertainty from resolution of uncertainty.**

---

# The central reading question

The record should ultimately be approached with a recurring question:

> **What was known here, what remained uncertain, what genuinely new evidence entered, what decision followed, and did that new evidence require the earlier formulation or management architecture to be reconsidered?**

That question should be asked repeatedly rather than answered once for the whole record.

---

# Closing note

`complete-record.md` can support more than one reasonable reading because it contains genuine structural pathology, a well-established neurological syndrome, changing treatment-state evidence, differing patient and clinician perspectives, explicit uncertainty, later reinterpretation, and a management strategy that evolved over years.

The purpose of this guide is therefore not to tell a reader **what conclusion to reach**.

Its purpose is to ensure that any conclusion reached is based on the correct evidential level, the correct point in time, the actual question asked of each service, and the distinction between what the record proves, what it suggests, and what remains a matter for expert judgment.
