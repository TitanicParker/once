# The clinical argument

> **Core argument:** the main longitudinal weakness was not necessarily the original decision to be cautious with levodopa. It was the failure to repeatedly re-test whether accumulating patient-specific evidence — especially medication-state changes in the feet and lower limbs — required the clinical model and treatment architecture to change.

## The central issue

The records do not support a simple account in which doctors “missed Parkinson’s” or every episode of foot pain had one neurological cause.

The patient had genuine structural foot problems and a very young-onset movement disorder with lower-limb dystonia, abnormal tone, a striking gait and marked medication sensitivity. Clinicians initially recorded the relationship between those two things as uncertain. The issue is whether that uncertainty was adequately revisited as further evidence accumulated.

Over time, lower-limb and foot symptoms repeatedly changed with neurological medication state. The patient also showed problems at both ends of the dopaminergic range: excessive stimulation could produce dyskinesia, while inadequate or badly timed treatment could leave him shuffling and in pain. In 2025, extending levodopa coverage overnight was followed by what the later record calls “profound relief” of severe morning foot pain.

In 2026, genetic testing confirmed biallelic PRKN/Parkin disease causing the parkinsonism and dystonia. That result cannot be treated as knowledge clinicians possessed years earlier. PARK2/PRKN, however, had already been specifically suspected by a specialist in 2017.

The clinical argument concerns **failure to re-synthesise accumulating evidence**. Initial caution about dopamine replacement may have been reasonable. Treating structural foot disease as real was also reasonable. Those early working positions appear to have remained more stable than the evidence around them.

## 1. The feet were abnormal before the neurological diagnosis

Before the first specialist neurological assessment, the records describe bilateral foot pain, abnormal gait, mallet toes, callouses and pressure areas. Those are genuine structural findings. They justify Podiatry involvement and should not be dismissed retrospectively.

There were also neurological signs: difficulty initiating movement, muscle tension, increased tone, brisk reflexes, toe clawing and progressive problems with walking. When Neurology examined the patient, the gait was described as strikingly abnormal and the lower-limb posture looked dystonic. There was rigidity and gross bradykinesia. The patient was extraordinarily young for a conventional Parkinson’s presentation.

The findings did not require a choice between “structural” and “neurological.” Both could be present. Structural lesions could hurt while abnormal tone, dystonic posture and altered loading influenced how the feet were used.

Neurology recorded the relationship as “Not clear.” That was a reasonable starting position. The question is whether “Not clear” remained an active question or gradually became a passive conclusion.

## 2. The 2017 specialist opinion

The unusual nature of the case was recognised early.

The treating neurologist considered recessively inherited Parkinsonism and acknowledged that he was not a movement-disorders specialist. A second opinion was sought from Prof Daniel Healy. The referral raised genetics, possible specialist review and the strategy of holding back levodopa.

Prof Healy’s September 2017 opinion recorded that the patient “probably has Park II,” recommended PARK2 testing, noted dramatic improvement on procyclidine and rasagiline, and described the treatment problem: a patient of this type might do very well on relatively low doses of dopamine replacement but could become dyskinetic if over-treated.

The patient appears to have had a narrow therapeutic window. Excess treatment could cause adverse effects; inadequate or badly timed treatment could also cause problems. The policy of restraint therefore needed to remain open to reconsideration as the patient's own response data accumulated.

## 3. The procyclidine withdrawal

An important change in the evidence occurred during the attempt to withdraw procyclidine.

Withdrawal was not designed as an experiment on the feet, but it provided relevant clinical information. As procyclidine was reduced, neurological tension worsened, the feet became restless and gait deteriorated. The treating neurologist later recorded the rapid return of the focal outside-left-foot/corn phenomenon when the drug was withdrawn, alongside the broader deterioration off treatment.

This did **not** prove that Parkinsonism caused a corn or that every foot symptom was dystonia. It showed that at least part of the lower-limb/foot syndrome was **modifiable by neurological medication state**.

That evidence changed the original “Not clear” position. Neurological treatment state was affecting the same symptom domain. The feet could therefore serve as a clinically relevant movement-disorders outcome, alongside their genuine structural pathology.

## 4. Treatment risk and foot response

Clinicians correctly retained evidence that stronger dopaminergic treatment could produce adverse effects. Higher-dose dopamine-agonist treatment was associated with dyskinesia or chorea and behavioural concerns. Later levodopa also required careful handling. The difficulty was described as a “tight rope.”

Those risks properly influenced future treatment.

The early evidence that the feet and lower limbs changed with neurological medication does not appear to have retained the same force across the treatment history. It was documented, but later records do not consistently show it being carried forward as a central premise when severe foot problems re-emerged.

Clinical reasoning needed to update in both directions. Evidence of harm constrained treatment; evidence of benefit or medication-state dependence also needed to influence interpretation and testing. A model focused on avoiding treatment peaks could miss clinically important troughs.

## 5. The 2021 substitution

In 2021, procyclidine was removed because of ocular concerns and Sinemet was introduced or increased as the replacement.

By then, procyclidine had already provided evidence that lower-limb and foot symptoms could change with neurological medication state. Replacing it with levodopa created a specific clinical question:

**Did the replacement adequately control the symptom domain that had previously shown medication sensitivity, especially the feet?**

The record does not clearly demonstrate a structured foot-specific substitution test.

That does not establish that Sinemet failed. It means an important prediction was not clearly tested. Structured observation of medication timing, foot symptoms, toe curling, gait and pain could have helped distinguish under-coverage, excessive treatment, structural symptoms and mixed mechanisms.

## 6. Medication timing in 2022–2023

In 2022, the feet were described as needing roughly 20 to 25 minutes to “warm up” in the morning. Sinemet use was relatively flexible. That was a clue rather than a conclusion.

By 2023, pain and shuffling were reported as worse when Sinemet had not been taken. Neurology interpreted the symptoms as OFF-related, and medication strategy was adjusted partly on that basis.

The relevant question was no longer simply whether the patient should receive more levodopa:

**Was he being overexposed at peaks and under-covered at troughs?**

Stronger dopaminergic stimulation could provoke dyskinesia, while low or delayed dopaminergic states could coincide with pain, shuffling and impaired movement. Timing, formulation and duration could therefore matter as much as total daily dose.

## 7. Pregabalin and mixed mechanisms

Pregabalin materially reduced the pain. That response does not establish that the pain was purely neuropathic or unrelated to the movement disorder. Toe curling and clawing could persist, and later pain still showed a relationship to dopaminergic state.

The record is compatible with a **mixed syndrome**: structural foot disease, motor and postural abnormalities, pain responsive to pregabalin, and later pain and motor difficulty varying with Parkinson medication state.

Those findings can coexist. Their coexistence required periodic re-synthesis rather than assignment of the symptoms to a single category.

## 8. Overnight coverage in 2025

By 2025 the severe morning pain had a clearer temporal pattern.

There was a long overnight gap in dopaminergic coverage. The feet improved after Parkinson medication entered the system. Controlled-release Sinemet was introduced to extend overnight coverage. The later record describes “profound relief.”

This does not prove that every earlier episode had the same mechanism, that controlled-release Sinemet should have been prescribed years earlier, or that there was negligence. It shows that **duration and timing of dopaminergic coverage could materially change the severe foot-pain state**.

The management problem was therefore not simply “too little levodopa” or “too much levodopa.” The record supports a narrow therapeutic window in which excessive stimulation and inadequate coverage could both cause difficulty.

## 9. Referral and specialist review

The record shows substantial multidisciplinary involvement, including Physiotherapy, Occupational Therapy, Podiatry, Occupational Health, Parkinson’s specialist nursing and Prof Healy. The issue is not the absence of referrals but their scope.

Podiatry addressed the structural feet. Physiotherapy and Occupational Therapy addressed function. Occupational Health addressed work capacity. The Parkinson’s nurse supported medication management. Prof Healy addressed genetics, PARK2 and the broad levodopa strategy.

The surviving record does not clearly demonstrate a later specialist referral framed around the integrated question:

**How should longstanding foot pain, toe clawing, dystonic posture, structural pathology and medication-state responsiveness be understood together in an unusually young, treatment-sensitive PARK2/PRKN-like disorder?**

That question acquired greater significance as dyskinesia, medication toxicity, pain, OFF phenomena and dose-timing problems accumulated.

## 10. The 2026 genetic result

The eventual genetic result confirmed two pathogenic Parkin variants and attributed the parkinsonism and dystonia to compound heterozygous PRKN/Parkin mutations.

This supports the significance of the PARK2 hypothesis raised in 2017, but it cannot be projected backwards as genetic certainty and does not make every historical foot lesion neurological. Clinicians had nevertheless identified a clinically meaningful hypothesis years before confirmation.

The intervening treatment-response evidence therefore remains relevant to whether the working model should have been revisited.

## Conclusion

A reasonable early model became progressively less adequate as new evidence accumulated.

Initially, it was reasonable to recognise genuine structural foot disease, remain uncertain about its relationship to Parkinsonism, and use caution with dopamine replacement in a very young patient at risk of dyskinesia.

Subsequent evidence included:

- a dystonic and Parkinsonian lower-limb phenotype;
- prospective suspicion of PARK2/PRKN;
- marked neurological treatment responsiveness;
- foot and gait deterioration during procyclidine withdrawal;
- treatment-limiting dyskinesia and behavioural effects;
- a therapeutic substitution from procyclidine to Sinemet that was not clearly validated against the feet;
- morning “warm-up” phenomena;
- pain and shuffling associated with missed or delayed Sinemet;
- meaningful pain benefit from pregabalin without resolution of all motor/postural abnormalities; and
- profound relief after overnight dopaminergic coverage was extended.

Together, these facts support a criticism of the longitudinal reasoning: **evidence of treatment risk was carried forward strongly, while evidence that the feet themselves were neurologically state-sensitive was not always given equivalent longitudinal weight.**

The patient may have been vulnerable to dyskinesia when dopaminergic stimulation was too high and to disabling motor and pain phenomena when coverage was too low or poorly distributed.

The expert question is:

> **At what points should the accumulating patient-specific evidence have triggered a formal re-evaluation of the foot syndrome, the timing and formulation of dopaminergic treatment, and the need for renewed movement-disorders specialist input?**

The documents alone cannot establish that earlier levodopa would certainly have relieved the pain, that controlled-release Sinemet should have been prescribed years earlier, that all foot pain was dystonic, or that any clinician breached the standard of care. Those questions require independent expert opinion.

The documentary argument is narrower: **the original caution may have been reasonable, but accumulating treatment-response evidence required repeated testing of the clinical model and treatment architecture.**
