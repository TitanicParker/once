# The clinical argument in plain English

> **Core argument:** the main longitudinal weakness was not necessarily the original decision to be cautious with levodopa. It was the failure to repeatedly re-test whether accumulating patient-specific evidence — especially medication-state changes in the feet and lower limbs — required the clinical model and treatment architecture to change.

This is the landing argument for the publication. The supporting evidence is organised separately so the reader can move from synthesis to source material without losing the thread.

## The central point

This case is not strongest if it is presented as a story in which doctors simply “missed Parkinson’s” or in which every episode of foot pain had one neurological cause. The records support something more careful, and in many ways more important.

The patient had genuine structural foot problems, but also a very young-onset movement disorder with lower-limb dystonia, abnormal tone, a striking gait and marked medication sensitivity. Clinicians initially recorded the relationship between those two things as uncertain. The key question is what happened after that uncertainty was recorded.

Over time, the lower-limb and foot symptoms repeatedly changed with neurological medication state. The patient also showed problems at both ends of the dopaminergic range: excessive stimulation could produce dyskinesia, while inadequate or badly timed treatment could leave him shuffling and in pain. In 2025, extending levodopa coverage overnight was followed by what the later record calls “profound relief” of severe morning foot pain.

In 2026, genetic testing finally confirmed biallelic PRKN/Parkin disease causing the parkinsonism and dystonia. That later result cannot be treated as knowledge that clinicians possessed years earlier. But it does matter when looking back at the clinical reasoning, because PARK2/PRKN was not a theory invented after the event. It had already been specifically suspected by a specialist in 2017.

The strongest clinical argument is therefore about **failure to re-synthesise accumulating evidence**. Initial caution about dopamine replacement may have been reasonable. Treating structural foot disease as real was also reasonable. The problem is that these early working positions appear to have remained more stable than the evidence around them.

## 1. The feet were abnormal before the neurological diagnosis

The story begins with the feet and gait already being clinically important.

Before the first specialist neurological assessment, the records describe bilateral foot pain, abnormal gait, mallet toes, callouses and pressure areas. Those are genuine structural findings. They justify Podiatry involvement and should not be dismissed retrospectively.

But at the same time, there were neurological warning signs: difficulty initiating movement, muscle tension, increased tone, brisk reflexes, toe clawing and progressive problems with walking. When Neurology examined the patient, the gait was described as strikingly abnormal and the lower-limb posture looked dystonic. There was rigidity and gross bradykinesia. The patient was also extraordinarily young for a conventional Parkinson’s presentation.

The proper model was never necessarily “structural” or “neurological.” Both could be true. Structural lesions could hurt while abnormal tone, dystonic posture and altered loading influenced how the feet were used.

Neurology recorded the relationship as “Not clear.” That was a reasonable starting position. The later issue is whether “Not clear” remained an active question or gradually became a passive conclusion.

## 2. The 2017 specialist opinion made this an unusually important phenotype

The unusual nature of the case was recognised early.

The treating neurologist considered recessively inherited Parkinsonism and acknowledged that he was not a movement-disorders specialist. A second opinion was sought from Prof Daniel Healy. The referral specifically raised genetics, possible specialist review and the strategy of holding back levodopa.

Prof Healy’s September 2017 opinion is important because he thought the patient “probably has Park II,” recommended PARK2 testing, noted dramatic improvement on procyclidine and rasagiline, and described the characteristic treatment problem: a patient of this type might do very well on relatively low doses of dopamine replacement but could become dyskinetic if over-treated.

The patient was not simply someone who needed “more dopamine.” He appears to have had a narrow therapeutic window: too much could cause trouble, but too little or badly timed treatment could also cause trouble. The serious question is whether the policy of restraint was repeatedly reconsidered as his own response data accumulated.

## 3. The procyclidine withdrawal changed the evidential position

One of the most important events occurred during the attempt to withdraw procyclidine.

Withdrawal was not designed as an experiment on the feet. But clinically it became one.

As procyclidine was reduced, neurological tension worsened, the feet became restless and gait deteriorated. The treating neurologist later recorded the rapid return of the focal outside-left-foot/corn phenomenon when the drug was withdrawn, alongside the broader deterioration off treatment.

This did **not** prove that Parkinsonism caused a corn. It did not prove that every foot symptom was dystonia. It did something more modest but still clinically important: it showed that at least part of the lower-limb/foot syndrome was **modifiable by neurological medication state**.

That should have changed the status of the original “Not clear” formulation. There was now patient-specific evidence that neurological treatment state affected the same symptom domain. The feet had become not just a Podiatry problem but a potentially useful movement-disorders outcome measure.

## 4. Evidence of treatment risk was remembered better than evidence of foot benefit

The record then develops an important asymmetry.

Clinicians correctly remembered that stronger dopaminergic treatment could produce adverse effects. Higher-dose dopamine-agonist treatment was associated with dyskinesia or chorea and behavioural concerns. Later levodopa also had to be handled carefully. The difficulty was explicitly described as a “tight rope.”

Those risks were real. They properly influenced future treatment.

But the early evidence that the feet and lower limbs changed with neurological medication does not appear to have retained the same force across the whole treatment history. It was documented locally, yet the later records do not consistently show it being carried forward as a central premise when severe foot problems re-emerged.

This is the heart of the argument. Clinical reasoning should update in both directions: evidence of harm should constrain treatment, while evidence of benefit or medication-state dependence should influence interpretation and testing. Otherwise the model can become biased toward avoiding peaks without recognising troughs.

## 5. The 2021 switch created a question that needed to be tested

In 2021, procyclidine was removed because of ocular concerns and Sinemet was introduced or increased as the replacement.

This was a major therapeutic junction.

By then, procyclidine had already produced evidence that lower-limb and foot symptoms could change with neurological medication state. Replacing it with levodopa therefore created a straightforward clinical question:

**Did the replacement adequately control the symptom domain that had previously shown medication sensitivity — especially the feet?**

The record does not clearly demonstrate a structured foot-specific substitution test.

That does not mean Sinemet failed. It means an important prediction was left insufficiently tested.

A structured longitudinal test of medication timing, foot symptoms, toe curling, gait and pain could potentially have clarified whether the patient was under-covered, over-treated, structurally symptomatic, or experiencing a mixture. It could also have refined the causal model.

## 6. By 2022–2023, the timing signal was becoming difficult to ignore

In 2022, the feet were described as needing roughly 20 to 25 minutes to “warm up” in the morning. Sinemet use was relatively flexible. On its own, that was only a clue. Later evidence became stronger.

By 2023, pain and shuffling were reported as worse when Sinemet had not been taken. Neurology itself interpreted the symptoms as OFF-related, and medication strategy was adjusted partly on that basis.

At that point the clinical question was no longer simply whether the patient should receive more levodopa.

The more useful question was:

**Was he being overexposed at peaks and under-covered at troughs?**

That formulation fits both sides of the record. Stronger dopaminergic stimulation could provoke dyskinesia, but low or delayed dopaminergic states could coincide with pain, shuffling and impaired movement.

This is a treatment-architecture problem: timing, formulation and duration may matter as much as total daily dose.

## 7. Pregabalin helped, but it did not settle the cause

Pregabalin materially reduced the pain, but that does not prove the pain was purely neuropathic or unrelated to the movement disorder. Toe curling and clawing could persist, and later pain still showed a relationship to dopaminergic state.

The most coherent reading is therefore a **mixed syndrome**.

There was structural foot disease. There were motor and postural abnormalities. There was pain that responded to an analgesic/neuropathic-pain agent. And there was later pain and motor difficulty that varied with Parkinson medication state.

Those findings are not mutually exclusive. Mixed mechanisms are exactly why the clinical model needed periodic re-synthesis rather than forcing the symptoms into a single category.

## 8. The 2025 overnight intervention finally tested the timing hypothesis directly

By 2025 the severe morning pain had a clearer temporal pattern.

There was a long overnight gap in dopaminergic coverage. The feet improved after Parkinson medication entered the system. Controlled-release Sinemet was then introduced specifically to extend overnight coverage.

The later record describes the result as “profound relief.”

This must be used carefully. It does not prove that every earlier episode had the same mechanism, that controlled-release Sinemet should have been prescribed years earlier, or that there was negligence. It does show that **duration and timing of dopaminergic coverage were capable of materially changing the severe foot-pain state**.

That result strongly supports the idea that the eventual management problem was not simply “too little levodopa” or “too much levodopa.” It was a narrow and complex therapeutic window in which the patient could suffer from both excessive stimulation and inadequate coverage.

## 9. The referral issue is about the question referred, not the number of referrals

The record shows substantial multidisciplinary involvement, including Physiotherapy, Occupational Therapy, Podiatry, Occupational Health, Parkinson’s specialist nursing and Prof Healy. So the strongest argument is not that nobody referred the patient or that specialist help was refused.

The better question is: **what problem was each referral actually asked to solve?**

Podiatry addressed the structural feet. Physiotherapy and Occupational Therapy addressed function. Occupational Health addressed work capacity. The Parkinson’s nurse supported medication management. Prof Healy addressed genetics, PARK2 and the broad levodopa strategy.

What is not clearly demonstrated in the surviving record is a later specialist referral framed around the integrated question:

**How should longstanding foot pain, toe clawing, dystonic posture, structural pathology and medication-state responsiveness be understood together in an unusually young, treatment-sensitive PARK2/PRKN-like disorder?**

That question became more important, not less, as dyskinesia, medication toxicity, pain, OFF phenomena and dose-timing problems accumulated.

## 10. What the 2026 genetic result does — and does not — prove

The eventual genetic result confirmed two pathogenic Parkin variants and attributed the parkinsonism and dystonia to compound heterozygous PRKN/Parkin mutations.

This validates the importance of the PARK2 hypothesis raised in 2017, but it cannot be projected backwards as genetic certainty, and it does not make every historical foot lesion neurological. Its significance is narrower: clinicians had identified a meaningful hypothesis early, and later testing confirmed that the phenotype was real.

That makes the intervening treatment-response evidence more important when assessing whether the working model should have been revisited.

## The strongest conclusion

The strongest clinical argument is not that one early decision was obviously wrong.

It is that a reasonable early model became progressively less adequate as new evidence accumulated.

Initially, it was reasonable to recognise genuine structural foot disease, to remain uncertain about its relationship to Parkinsonism, and to be cautious with dopamine replacement in a very young patient at risk of dyskinesia.

But the evidence did not stand still.

The patient then demonstrated:

- a dystonic and Parkinsonian lower-limb phenotype;
- prospective suspicion of PARK2/PRKN;
- marked neurological treatment responsiveness;
- foot and gait deterioration during procyclidine withdrawal;
- later treatment-limiting dyskinesia and behavioural effects;
- a therapeutic substitution from procyclidine to Sinemet that was not clearly validated against the feet;
- morning “warm-up” phenomena;
- pain and shuffling associated with missed or delayed Sinemet;
- meaningful pain benefit from pregabalin without resolution of all motor/postural abnormalities; and
- profound relief after overnight dopaminergic coverage was extended.

Taken together, those facts support a coherent criticism of the longitudinal reasoning: **evidence of treatment risk was carried forward strongly, while evidence that the feet themselves were neurologically state-sensitive was not always given equivalent longitudinal weight.**

That matters because the patient may have been caught between peaks and troughs — vulnerable to dyskinesia when dopaminergic stimulation was too high, yet vulnerable to disabling motor and pain phenomena when coverage was too low or poorly distributed.

The unresolved expert question is therefore not simply, “Should levodopa have been started earlier?”

It is:

> **At what points should the accumulating patient-specific evidence have triggered a formal re-evaluation of the foot syndrome, the timing and formulation of dopaminergic treatment, and the need for renewed movement-disorders specialist input?**

That is the question the documentary record is strongest in asking.

The limits of the record must also be respected. The documents alone cannot establish that earlier levodopa would certainly have relieved the pain, that controlled-release Sinemet should have been prescribed years earlier, that all foot pain was dystonic, or that any clinician breached the standard of care. Those are matters for independent expert opinion.

What the record can show is the evolution of the evidence. That evolution supports a clear central argument: **the major longitudinal weakness was not necessarily the original caution, but the failure to repeatedly re-test whether the patient’s accumulating treatment-response evidence required the clinical model and treatment architecture to change.**
