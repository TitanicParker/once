## Executive summary

In my clinical reading of the complete longitudinal record, **this was not primarily a case in which the original diagnosis was missed or in which one simple cause of foot pain was overlooked. It was a case in which an unusually young, treatment-sensitive parkinsonian disorder with prominent lower-limb dystonic features was recognised, but the implications of that phenotype—and especially the implications of the patient's own treatment-response evidence—were not consistently integrated into the management model over time.**

The eventual 2026 result confirms biallelic **PRKN/Parkin disease causing the parkinsonism and dystonia**. That later confirmation cannot be projected backwards as knowledge, but it validates the clinical importance of the PARK2 hypothesis that was already being considered in 2017. PRKN disease is characteristically early-onset, slowly progressive, may present with lower-limb dystonia and hyperreflexia, and is often highly levodopa responsive while also being prone to levodopa-induced dyskinesia. ([PubMed][1]) In this patient, Prof Healy prospectively thought he “probably has Park II,” recommended testing, recorded dramatic improvement on procyclidine/rasagiline, and described the characteristic problem of useful low-dose dopamine replacement coupled with dyskinesia if over-used. 

### What I think happened

**First, the treatment strategy was established very early around dopaminergic restraint.** The treating neurologist explicitly intended to be “extremely cautious” with levodopa/dopamine agonists and to “leave off on this for as long as possible.”  Importantly, this was not presented as a risk-free strategy. The same clinician recognised that levodopa might work very well and sustainably and expressly recorded that “we may regret that we did not start this earlier.” 

In my view, that creates the central longitudinal issue. **The initial caution itself is understandable; the question is whether it remained proportionate and sufficiently revisable as new patient-specific evidence accumulated.**

**Second, the foot syndrome was never satisfactorily reduced to one mechanism—and it should not now be reduced to one retrospectively.** There was genuine structural pathology: corns, calluses, clawing/hammertoes, pronation and mechanical pressure lesions were repeatedly documented. But there was also lower-limb dystonic phenomenology, abnormal tone and gait. Later PRKN confirmation strengthens attribution of the dystonia/parkinsonism, but it does not prove that every corn, burning sensation or painful episode was caused directly by PRKN. The findings register appropriately preserves these as parallel, potentially interacting processes rather than mutually exclusive explanations. 

**Third, the 2017 procyclidine withdrawal materially changed the evidential position.** Before it, Neurology could reasonably say that the relationship between the Parkinsonian disorder and the foot syndrome was “Not clear.” But the planned withdrawal generated a within-patient experiment: as procyclidine was withdrawn, neurological tension worsened, the feet became restless and gait deteriorated; by 2018 the neurologist had recorded rapid return of the focal outside-left-foot/corn phenomenon. The safe conclusion was not “Parkinsonism caused the corn.” It was that **at least part of the foot syndrome was neurologically medication-state modifiable.** The findings register itself identifies this as a re-synthesis trigger. 

That, in my opinion, was the first major missed **informational opportunity**. Once a longstanding foot disorder with dystonic features had shown medication-state sensitivity in a patient already suspected of PARK2, the feet were no longer merely an unresolved Podiatry problem. They had become a potentially informative movement-disorders outcome measure.

**Fourth, that early foot-specific evidence did not retain the same longitudinal force as evidence of treatment risk.** Dyskinesia, behavioural effects and later low-dose levodopa sensitivity were repeatedly remembered and allowed to constrain treatment. By contrast, the 2017–2018 foot-specific withdrawal evidence remained visible locally but was not clearly re-used when the same broad foot problem resurfaced years later. The findings register classifies this as local updating without global re-synthesis, and in the more focal 2018 finding, loss of visible evidential continuity. 

I regard that asymmetry as one of the most important features of the case.

**Fifth, the 2021 transition from procyclidine to Sinemet was the pivotal therapeutic junction.** Procyclidine was removed because of ocular concerns and immediate-release levodopa was introduced/increased as its replacement. But the record does not demonstrate that the replacement was specifically validated against the symptom domain that had previously generated its own treatment-response signal—the feet. In other words:

**a neurological drug with demonstrated foot-state effects was removed → levodopa was expected to compensate → there is no clear documented foot-specific substitution test.**

That does not establish that the replacement failed. It means an important clinical proposition remained untested.

**Sixth, the succeeding years generated exactly the sort of evidence that should have caused that proposition to be reopened.** In 2022 the feet repeatedly required 20–25 minutes to “warm up” in the morning while Sinemet use was relatively flexible. The register correctly treats this as a clue rather than proof of OFF pain.  By later 2023, however, the evidence had become stronger: pain and shuffling were reported as worse when Sinemet had not been taken, Neurology itself regarded them as OFF-related, and Stalevo was used partly because of that interpretation. 

At that point, in my opinion, the clinical question had changed. It was no longer simply:

**“Should we give more levodopa?”**

It was:

**“Is this patient simultaneously being overexposed at peaks and undercovered at troughs?”**

That distinction matters enormously because the record contains both phenomena: dyskinesia with stronger dopaminergic stimulation and pain/shuffling with lower dopaminergic states.

**Seventh, pregabalin was genuinely beneficial, but its benefit did not settle the mechanism.** It materially reduced pain and worsening followed major self-reduction, so its effect should not be discounted. But pain relief coexisted with persistent toe curling/clawing, and later pain remained sensitive to levodopa state. The most coherent interpretation is therefore a **mixed syndrome**: structural/mechanical pain plus neurological motor/postural phenomena and state-dependent pain modulation, rather than a simple choice between “neuropathic pain” and “Parkinson's pain.” The record specifically shows pain improving with pregabalin while toe-curling persisted. 

**Eighth, by 2025 the temporal-coverage hypothesis was finally tested directly.** Severe morning pain was associated with the long overnight gap; the feet improved after Parkinson medication entered the system; controlled-release Sinemet was then introduced to change the *duration and timing* of overnight coverage rather than merely increasing daytime dosing. The subsequent response was described as “profound relief.”  The register correctly treats that as evidence specifically about **timing/formulation/duration of dopaminergic coverage**, not proof of the original pain generator or proof that the identical intervention would have worked years earlier. 

To me, this is the strongest evidence that the eventual management problem was a **therapeutic-window problem**: too much dopaminergic stimulation could produce dyskinesia, but insufficient or poorly distributed stimulation could leave disabling OFF motor and pain phenomena exposed.

### The 2025 retrospective narrative

There is also a documentary issue that I think matters clinically.

In 2025 the consultant retrospectively described a relatively continuous story: that Parkinson's had been known, dopaminergic responsiveness had been known, Sinemet's effect on pain had been known, and pregabalin had been used partly to spare Sinemet. 

Parts of that account are well supported. The longstanding concern about dyskinesia and other dopaminergic complications was real and contemporaneous.

But the complete record is **less linear than that retrospective description**. PARK2 was a specific competing genetic formulation; the foot relationship remained explicitly unresolved; the 2021 Sinemet initiation record does not establish chronic foot pain as the reason for introducing it; and medication-state relevance to pain appears to have been re-established more explicitly through later 2023 observations. The findings register therefore reasonably characterises parts of the later narrative as retrospective reconstruction rather than treating the 2025 account as an authoritative description of what was continuously understood from 2017 onward. 

## My overall opinion

**I think this patient probably had a PRKN-related disorder in which lower-limb dystonia was an early and clinically important component, superimposed on genuine structural foot pathology. The painful foot syndrome was therefore likely mixed rather than unitary.**

**The initial levodopa caution was a defensible clinical concern, particularly given the patient's youth and subsequent demonstrated dyskinesia sensitivity. What became problematic from a reasoning standpoint was that the strategy of dopaminergic restraint appears to have remained more stable than the evidential environment around it.**

Patient-specific evidence progressively accumulated that:

* the feet participated in neurological medication-state change;
* PARK2 was a serious prospective diagnosis;
* the patient was both dopaminergically responsive and dyskinesia-sensitive;
* the procyclidine-to-Sinemet substitution had not clearly been validated against the feet;
* morning and end-of-dose phenomena emerged;
* later pain/shuffling were explicitly recognised as OFF-related; and
* extending overnight levodopa coverage ultimately produced profound relief.

The record itself identifies the same pattern: **treatment-risk evidence was carried forward strongly, while some early foot-specific benefit/modifiability evidence had local treatment force without equivalent longitudinal causal force.** 

So, if I had to state the case in one sentence:

> **This appears to have been a highly treatment-sensitive PRKN parkinsonism/dystonia in which a genuine structural foot disorder and a neurologically state-sensitive lower-limb syndrome became clinically entangled; the major longitudinal weakness was not necessarily the initial decision to defer levodopa, but the failure to repeatedly and explicitly re-test whether accumulating patient-specific evidence should revise the diagnostic interpretation of the feet and the architecture of the dopaminergic treatment strategy.**

What I **cannot** conclude from the record alone is that earlier levodopa would certainly have relieved the foot pain, that Sinemet CR should definitely have been prescribed in 2017–2022, that every historical pain episode was dystonic, or that the management amounted to negligence. Those are precisely the counterfactual and standard-of-care questions that require an independent movement-disorders expert.
