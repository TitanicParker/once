# Iterative Reading and Evidential Instability in the Case Record

## Purpose

This note records a methodological observation arising from an independent review of the case materials.

The important point is not that the underlying facts changed between readings. They did not.

What changed was the **reader's evidential model** of those facts.

On the first reading, the material could be understood in a relatively conventional longitudinal way: a young patient with Parkinsonism, a difficult treatment balance, recurrent foot pain with mixed structural and neurological features, gradual treatment evolution, and later major benefit from overnight dopaminergic coverage.

On a second, more structured reading — particularly after working through the **Record Findings Register** — the same facts became materially harder to compress into that simple narrative.

The second reading exposed distinctions, temporal limits, competing natural experiments, referral-scope problems, observation-window limitations, and losses of visible evidential continuity that were present in the record all along but were not equally salient on first encounter.

That difference between the first and second reading is itself important.

It demonstrates that this is a case in which **the order, method and depth of reading can materially affect the apparent clinical meaning of an unchanged factual record**.

---

# 1. What happened in the review process

The first assessment was based on the principal public-facing material and a substantial amount of the underlying clinical record.

It was not based on a full reading of the 11,598-line **Record Findings Register**.

That omission mattered.

The Register was explicitly designed to force separation between:

- case facts;
- facts about the record;
- interpretive consequences;
- counterevidence;
- unresolved propositions;
- decision points; and
- questions that properly require external expert judgment.

It also imposes controls against several forms of retrospective over-reading:

- absence of documentation is not proof that something did not happen;
- a later explanation is not proof that the same rationale existed contemporaneously;
- treatment response is not proof of mechanism;
- structural pathology, neurological motor phenomena, pain generation, pain modulation and functional consequence must not be collapsed into one proposition;
- a fact may lose visible evidential continuity in later notes without proving that it was forgotten or ignored.

Those controls materially changed the way the same clinical events had to be interpreted.

The first and second readings therefore differed **not because new primary clinical facts appeared**, but because the second reading used a more explicit evidential architecture.

That distinction is fundamental.

---

# 2. Why the same facts looked different on the second reading

## 2.1 The first reading encouraged narrative compression

A long medical record naturally invites compression.

A reader tends to form a story:

1. Parkinsonism emerged.
2. Levodopa was deferred because of age and dyskinesia risk.
3. Other dopaminergic treatments were tried.
4. Foot pain remained difficult.
5. Levodopa was eventually introduced.
6. OFF symptoms later became clearer.
7. Sinemet CR eventually produced profound benefit.

All of those propositions are broadly grounded in the record.

But that sequence is **not sufficient** to determine whether management was reasonable at each stage.

The Register makes clear that the treatment policy itself changed over time. The case was not governed by one static “levodopa-sparing” strategy from 2017 to 2025.

The record instead supports identifiable phases:

- initial deferral of levodopa/dopamine agonists;
- reliance on rasagiline/procyclidine;
- dopamine-agonist treatment with a narrow therapeutic window;
- introduction of levodopa in 2021;
- minimisation of levodopa because of early dyskinesia;
- later demand/OFF-state use;
- timing and absorption adjustments;
- finally, explicit testing of overnight controlled-release coverage.

That means a fair negligence analysis cannot ask simply:

> “Was levodopa withheld too long?”

It must ask:

> “At which specific management phase did the evidential state change enough that the existing strategy ought to have been reconsidered?”

That is a much narrower and more difficult question.

---

## 2.2 The second reading made temporal reach much more important

The record contains later statements that are true or partly true in 2025 but reach backward into earlier years.

Examples include claims that:

- Sinemet had been known to help pain “for quite some time”;
- pregabalin had functioned as a levodopa-sparing treatment;
- dopaminergic responsiveness had been “known”;
- the overall management strategy was continuous.

The structured re-reading showed that these statements have different historical support.

For example:

- the record clearly supports a pain-related Sinemet/OFF relationship by later 2023;
- the 2021 initiation note does **not** show foot pain as the reason Sinemet was first started;
- pregabalin was originally GP-initiated for severe burning/neuropathic-type pain, while a levodopa-sparing function is much clearer later;
- the rationale for dopaminergic caution is strongly and repeatedly supported;
- but the treatment strategy itself evolved and should not be rewritten as one unchanged policy.

This changes how much weight can safely be placed on the 2025 complaint response as a historical account.

It is evidence of the clinician's later synthesis.

It is not automatically evidence that the same synthesis governed each earlier decision.

---

# 3. The most important example: the feet

The foot syndrome is especially vulnerable to oversimplification.

A first-pass reader can easily end up choosing one of several competing narratives:

- structural foot pain;
- neuropathic pain;
- Parkinson-related pain;
- painful dystonia;
- OFF-related pain;
- psychological amplification;
- central pain modulation.

The record does not justify reducing the syndrome to any one of these.

The stronger reading is that several mechanisms could coexist.

The record contains genuine evidence for:

- recurrent structural lesions and pressure pathology;
- clawing/toe deformity;
- abnormal posture and dystonic-looking gait;
- medication-state-sensitive changes;
- burning/neuropathic-type pain;
- substantial pregabalin responsiveness;
- later OFF-related pain/shuffling;
- pain reduction with levodopa;
- profound improvement after altered overnight coverage;
- continued uncertainty about the underlying pain generator.

The Register therefore forces a distinction between:

**pain generation**  
and  
**pain modulation**.

That is a major shift in interpretation.

A structural corn or local mechanical lesion may generate pain.

Parkinsonian/dystonic state, altered loading, central pain processing or OFF state may amplify or reduce that pain.

A dopaminergic treatment response can therefore be clinically important without proving that Parkinsonism caused the original lesion or every painful symptom.

---

# 4. A fact that became more important on re-reading: 2017–2018 medication-state evidence

The 2017 procyclidine withdrawal/rechallenge is one of the clearest examples of how the second reading changed the evidential emphasis.

The contemporaneous record documented:

- substantial neurological improvement on treatment;
- worsening tension and gait during withdrawal;
- restless or worsening foot symptoms while off treatment;
- improvement in vision while off treatment;
- reintroduction because the lower-treatment state was not tolerable.

In 2018 the treating neurologist recorded a more focal feature: the outside-left-foot/corn phenomenon returned rapidly during the withdrawal.

The safe conclusion is not that the corn itself was “caused by Parkinson's.”

The stronger conclusion is that **the foot symptom complex had become demonstrably medication-state sensitive**.

That was new patient-specific evidence.

The crucial later issue is that this fact had immediate local treatment force, but its full significance is not visibly carried into later recurrences of focal foot pain.

The Register classifies this as a problem of **evidential continuity**, not proof of negligence.

That distinction is important.

A later note failing to mention an earlier fact does not prove that the clinician forgot it.

But it does mean an expert can legitimately ask:

> When recurrent severe foot pain returned, should that earlier medication-state evidence have been explicitly re-integrated into the working model?

That question is substantially more precise than alleging that the foot problem was simply “missed.”

---

# 5. The strongest defence became clearer on the second reading

The second reading did not simply make the criticism stronger.

It also materially strengthened the defence.

The record contains unusually strong evidence that the patient had a **narrow dopaminergic therapeutic window**.

This is not merely a theoretical concern arising from young age.

It became patient-specific:

- higher Neupro exposure produced dyskinesia/choreiform effects;
- behavioural/impulsivity concerns appeared;
- 10 mg could feel insufficient;
- low-dose Sinemet later produced early choreiform movement;
- Stalevo 75 produced more obvious dyskinesia;
- later treatment decisions continued to be constrained by that history.

This makes it much harder to frame the case as:

> “There was an obvious effective treatment and the clinicians simply failed to give enough of it.”

The record supports a genuine undertreatment-versus-adverse-effect problem.

That is a major defence point.

It also explains why the stronger criticism, if one exists, is likely to concern **therapeutic design** rather than simple dose escalation.

In other words:

- timing;
- formulation;
- duration of coverage;
- meal relation;
- structured OFF assessment;
- symptom-specific targeting;

may be more important than the crude question of “more levodopa.”

---

# 6. The 2017 levodopa issue looks weaker after structured re-reading

The 2017 decision is not an easy breach point.

The record shows:

- severe functional impairment;
- very young age;
- concern about dyskinesia and impulse-control effects;
- acknowledgement that levodopa might work very well;
- acknowledgement that later regret about delay was possible;
- good response to procyclidine/rasagiline;
- specialist input from Prof Healy;
- Healy's view that probable PARK2 disease could do well on relatively low-dose dopamine replacement but could become dyskinetic if over-used.

Importantly, Healy did **not** simply direct that levodopa be started immediately.

That makes a simple 2017 “failure to prescribe levodopa” allegation difficult.

The more defensible question is whether the original temporary deferral policy was **revisited at the right later thresholds**.

---

# 7. Where the case becomes more concerning

The structured reading shifts attention away from 2017 as the strongest potential breach period and toward the later evolution of the case.

The potentially important thresholds include:

## 2018–2019

By this stage:

- the foot had shown medication-state sensitivity;
- procyclidine benefit was substantial but toxicity-limited;
- amantadine had failed;
- Neupro produced a narrow benefit/adverse-effect window;
- the patient reported later-day deterioration that was not always represented by clinic examination.

This is a legitimate reassessment point.

But the defence remains strong because dopaminergic adverse effects were real and the patient could sometimes appear near-normal in clinic.

## 2021–2022

Levodopa was introduced.

The patient then showed early levodopa sensitivity.

A reproducible morning foot “warm-up” pattern appeared in 2022.

But at that time:

- overall function could be very good;
- the patient was walking for hours;
- cramping was near absent;
- soreness was still described around known corns/calluses;
- the OFF mechanism had not yet been clearly established.

The 2022 pattern is therefore a clue, not proof that controlled-release levodopa should already have been prescribed.

## Later 2023 onward

This is where the case becomes materially stronger.

By later 2023:

- pain and shuffling were reported to worsen when Sinemet had not been taken;
- a clinician explicitly described the leg pain/shuffling as OFF-related;
- Stalevo was started on that basis;
- levodopa was reported to help function and pain;
- evening deterioration remained an issue;
- dyskinesia continued to limit conventional escalation.

At this point, pain had entered the dopaminergic management architecture as an explicit treatment target.

The key expert question becomes:

> Once OFF-related pain/shuffling was explicit, should medication timing, formulation, duration of action and overnight coverage have been explored more systematically and earlier?

That is a much stronger and more defensible issue than a broad allegation that levodopa should simply have been started years earlier.

---

# 8. The 2024 record is particularly important

The 2024 record is easy to underestimate on a first read.

It shows:

- major pain benefit from pregabalin;
- persistent toe curling despite that pain improvement;
- residual evening limitation;
- ongoing dyskinesia concerns;
- an attempt to change evening Sinemet timing relative to food and fluid.

This has two implications.

First, it supports a **mixed syndrome**.

Pain and motor/postural phenomena were not moving together.

Second, it shows that timing was already recognised as a therapeutic variable before 2025.

That weakens any allegation that the clinicians simply failed to think about timing.

The narrower question is whether the **specific timing/coverage problem** was adequately tested.

The outcome of the 2024 timing change is not clearly documented.

That is a material factual gap.

---

# 9. The 2025 Sinemet CR response: strong evidence, but only for a bounded proposition

The later profound response to Sinemet CR is important.

But its evidential scope must be carefully controlled.

It strongly supports the proposition that:

> the morning foot-pain experience was materially influenced by dopaminergic timing, formulation and duration of overnight coverage.

It does **not** prove:

- that every historical foot pain episode was dopaminergic;
- that the underlying structural lesions were caused by Parkinsonism;
- that peripheral neuropathy was absent;
- that painful dystonia was the sole mechanism;
- that the same CR strategy would definitely have worked in 2017, 2018, 2021 or 2022;
- that earlier non-use of CR was negligent.

The 2025 response closes **efficacy in that observed treatment state** much more strongly than it closes historical mechanism or counterfactual causation.

---

# 10. Why this case is especially difficult to assess in one reading

This record contains several features that systematically increase the risk of first-pass error.

## 10.1 Multiple causal levels occupy the same anatomy

The same foot can simultaneously contain:

- a structural lesion;
- abnormal posture;
- altered loading;
- dystonia;
- neuropathic-type symptoms;
- centrally modulated pain;
- OFF-related worsening.

A reader who asks only “what caused the foot pain?” is already using a question that may be too coarse.

## 10.2 Treatment response is non-monotonic

The patient is neither simply responsive nor unresponsive to dopaminergic therapy.

More treatment can improve one domain and worsen another.

That makes retrospective reasoning difficult.

## 10.3 Clinic observation is state-dependent

There are occasions where the patient looked relatively good in clinic but reported being worse later.

The record itself acknowledges this.

Therefore:

> “Looked well in clinic”

cannot be treated as equivalent to:

> “Was adequately treated throughout the medication cycle.”

## 10.4 Later documents are narratively stronger than earlier documents

Complaint responses and later summaries often sound more coherent because they are written after years of accumulated experience.

That coherence can be misleading if allowed to back-project into earlier periods.

## 10.5 Some early facts lose visible force

The early foot medication-state evidence is documented and acted on locally but is not always visibly re-used years later.

By contrast, adverse-effect evidence is repeatedly and explicitly carried forward.

That asymmetry is potentially important.

## 10.6 New evidence sometimes increased uncertainty

The March 2023 crisis did not make the case clearer.

It introduced:

- severe burning pain;
- variable pregabalin response;
- a patient report of no obvious difference after ten days off Parkinson medication;
- GP recollection of earlier improvement with Parkinson control.

This widened the evidential conflict.

Later 2023 then supplied new OFF-linked evidence.

A reader who collapses the whole of 2023 into one narrative misses that progression.

---

# 11. What changed in my assessment after the second reading

The second reading did **not** make me conclude that the site’s central case theory was proved.

It made the case more nuanced.

The principal changes were:

### A. I became less persuaded by a simple “levodopa should have been started in 2017” theory.

The contemporaneous risk-benefit rationale is real and repeatedly supported.

### B. I became more interested in the later question of re-synthesis.

The stronger concern is whether accumulated evidence — especially by later 2023 — should have triggered more systematic reassessment of:

- OFF symptoms;
- dose versus timing;
- immediate-release versus controlled-release formulation;
- overnight coverage;
- foot-specific medication-state response;
- dystonia/postural contribution.

### C. I gave more weight to the defence based on patient-specific dyskinesia sensitivity.

This was not merely defensive retrospective language.

It was empirically demonstrated.

### D. I gave more weight to the 2017–2018 foot medication-state evidence.

Not because it proves mechanism, but because it made neurological modifiability of the foot symptom complex knowable much earlier than 2025.

### E. I became more cautious about retrospective complaint-response language.

Some of it is well supported.

Some of it is broader than the contemporaneous record.

### F. I became more convinced that causation is harder than breach.

Even if an expert criticises delayed reassessment or delayed coverage testing, proving that earlier alternative management **probably** would have materially reduced the duration or severity of the entire pain/disability syndrome remains difficult because:

- structural pathology was real;
- pregabalin clearly helped;
- dopaminergic responses were variable;
- dyskinesia constrained treatment;
- the 2025 response does not prove earlier counterfactual success.

---

# 12. What this implies for independent expert review

An independent movement-disorders neurologist should not be asked to review this case as a single global proposition.

The expert should be required to answer discrete questions at specific dates.

For example:

1. Was the initial 2017 levodopa-deferral strategy within a reasonable range of practice?
2. What significance should have been assigned to the 2017 procyclidine withdrawal effect on gait and foot symptoms?
3. By 2018–2019, had the combination of residual symptoms and treatment toxicity created a need for a different dopaminergic strategy?
4. Did the 2022 morning foot pattern warrant structured medication-state assessment?
5. By later 2023, once pain and shuffling were expressly considered OFF-related, what timing/formulation strategies should reasonably have been considered?
6. Should a movement-disorders specialist have been re-involved at that stage?
7. Should the persistent toe-curling component have been separately assessed as dystonia/postural pathology?
8. When did overnight dopaminergic coverage become a reasonable treatment hypothesis?
9. Would an earlier trial of that strategy probably have improved pain/disability to a material degree?
10. How much of the overall pain syndrome was structural, dystonic, neuropathic, OFF-related or centrally modulated?

A single question such as “Was the patient negligently undertreated?” is too crude.

---

# 13. The methodological lesson

This case demonstrates a broader evidential phenomenon:

## **The first coherent narrative is not necessarily the most accurate narrative.**

Longitudinal medical records reward familiarity.

On first reading, the reader tends to organise facts around major diagnostic and treatment milestones.

On repeated reading, different kinds of facts become visible:

- temporal clues;
- discordant observations;
- state dependence;
- referral boundaries;
- inherited terminology;
- gaps in carry-forward;
- distinctions between treatment effect and mechanism;
- distinctions between pain and motor phenomena;
- distinctions between dose, timing, formulation and duration of action.

That does not mean repeated reading necessarily makes a case stronger.

It may strengthen the defence as much as the criticism.

What repeated reading does is reduce the risk of **premature narrative closure**.

---

# 14. Why the second reading was different even though the facts were the same

The most concise formulation is this:

> **The evidence did not change. The evidential relationships changed.**

The second reading made it easier to see:

- which facts supported the same proposition;
- which facts only appeared to support the same proposition;
- which were independent;
- which were copied or retrospective;
- which contradicted each other;
- which operated at different causal levels;
- which changed the therapeutic question without changing the diagnosis;
- which facts were acted on locally but not visibly integrated longitudinally;
- which later facts made earlier clues more salient without making them retrospectively decisive.

This is why a case of this kind cannot safely be assessed from one narrative reading alone.

---

# 15. Practical consequence for the medico-legal case

The record is unlikely to produce a reliable expert opinion if presented as:

> “Here is a long chronology. Was the care negligent?”

It is more likely to produce a reliable opinion if presented as:

> “Here are the specific evidential state changes and management decision points. At each point, what would a reasonably competent movement-disorders neurologist have done?”

That approach keeps:

- breach;
- causation;
- damages;
- mechanism;
- hindsight;
- and documentary interpretation

separate.

It also reduces the risk that either claimant or defendant narrative becomes the organising framework before the expert has independently understood the evidence.

---

# 16. A caution about the website itself

There is an additional methodological point.

The website necessarily imposes structure on a very complex record.

Any structure creates emphasis.

That does not make the website misleading.

But it means a reader can be led toward coherence before fully appreciating the degree of contradiction and unresolved uncertainty inside the source record.

The Register is valuable precisely because it partly counteracts that effect by forcing the reader to preserve:

- competing evidence;
- temporal limits;
- unresolved propositions;
- alternative mechanisms;
- and expert boundaries.

The fact that my second reading changed the weighting of the same facts is therefore not incidental.

It is evidence that the case is unusually sensitive to **presentation architecture and reading method**.

---

# 17. Bottom line

The experience of reading this case twice is itself worth recording.

On the first reading, the case can look like a dispute about whether levodopa was withheld for too long.

On the second structured reading, it looks more like a dispute about:

- how evolving evidence was synthesised;
- whether treatment-state clues at the feet were given sufficient longitudinal weight;
- whether the narrow therapeutic window was managed by sufficiently sophisticated use of dose, timing, formulation and coverage;
- whether mixed structural, dystonic and pain-modulatory mechanisms were adequately separated;
- and whether the point at which OFF-related pain became explicit should have triggered a more specialised or systematic reassessment.

That is a materially different case theory.

Yet the underlying clinical record is the same.

That is exactly why this case is difficult to convey in a single reading — and why any fair expert assessment should be explicitly iterative, proposition-specific and temporally controlled.
