NOTABLE / STRIKING STATEMENTS ACROSS THE RECORD


1. JULY 2017 — LEVODOPA DEFERRAL AND ANTICIPATED REGRET

QUOTE:

“I think sooner or later we may have to introduce these. It is possible that he will do very well with levodopa and in a sustainable way whenever it is started. Indeed in time to come — we may regret that we did not start this earlier.”

INTERESTING PERSPECTIVE:

This is one of the most important statements in the entire record.

The potential downside of delaying levodopa was not invented retrospectively by the patient in 2025. The treating neurologist expressly anticipated it at the outset.

It therefore creates a prospective management question:

If the clinician recognised in 2017 that the patient might do very well on levodopa and that delaying it might later be regretted, what subsequent patient-specific evidence was intended to trigger reconsideration of that strategy?

The significance is not that the original decision was necessarily wrong. It is that the original decision was explicitly recognised as a trade-off requiring later reassessment.


2. JULY 2017 — “NO DISADVANTAGE” FROM DELAY

QUOTE:

“Certainly, in no way will any individual be disadvantaged from the neuropathological or progression risk perspectives in terms of delaying commencement of the symptomatic treatments.”

INTERESTING PERSPECTIVE:

The qualification is crucial: “neuropathological or progression risk perspectives.”

That is not the same proposition as saying there can be no symptomatic, functional, pain-related or quality-of-life disadvantage from withholding symptomatic treatment.

Placed immediately after the statement that levodopa might work very well and that its delayed introduction might later be regretted, the wording actually highlights two different questions:

1. Does delaying levodopa accelerate the disease?
2. What symptoms and function might remain untreated while it is being delayed?

The first proposition does not answer the second.


3. 2017 — CORRESPONDENCE TO PROF DANIEL HEALY
   [Exact date not visible on the surviving reproduced page; before Healy’s response of 12 September 2017]

QUOTE:

“Myself and Tim are keen to hold back on Levodopa insofar as maybe possible unless you'd advise otherwise?”

INTERESTING PERSPECTIVE:

This is important because it shows that levodopa restraint was a deliberate management proposition put to the external specialist for comment.

But the wording also matters:

“unless you'd advise otherwise?”

The question for an expert is therefore what Prof Healy actually endorsed.

Healy subsequently described probable PARK2, dramatic improvement on the existing treatment, and the tendency of PARK2 patients to manage with relatively low doses of dopamine replacement while becoming dyskinetic if over-used.

That is not necessarily identical to an instruction to avoid levodopa altogether for as long as possible.

The distinction between “low-dose/cautious dopamine replacement” and “prolonged deferral” deserves careful examination.


4. 24 APRIL 2018 — THE FOOT CHANGES FIRST OFF PROCYCLIDINE

QUOTE:

“Our Nurse [Parkinson's Nurse] had suggested stopping Procyclidine for two weeks in Sept/Oct 2017 ... but when he did that — the first thing he noticed changing and changing rapidly was the return on the foot, of the outside left corn.”

INTERESTING PERSPECTIVE:

This is an unusually important observation because it is the treating neurologist's own record of the medication-withdrawal experiment.

It does not establish that Parkinsonism “caused the corn.”

What it establishes is narrower and potentially much more useful:

the focal foot phenomenon changed rapidly when neurological medication state changed.

That was new patient-specific evidence after the earlier conclusion that the relationship between the feet and Parkinsonism was “Not clear.”

The key question is whether this finding subsequently acquired durable clinical significance, or whether it was documented locally and then effectively ceased to influence the longitudinal model.


5. 24 APRIL 2018 — “OBVIOUSLY HE COULD BE VERY MUCH BETTER”

QUOTE:

“Obviously he could be very much better if we would actually give him the levodopa or another dopamine agonist — however for a very clearly set out reasons, we do not want to do that — at least for quite some time.”

INTERESTING PERSPECTIVE:

This is probably the strongest statement of the treatment trade-off after 2017.

The clinician is not saying:

“We do not know whether dopamine replacement would help.”

He is saying:

“Obviously he could be very much better…”

and nevertheless continuing the strategy of withholding it “for quite some time.”

That makes the subsequent question particularly sharp:

What evidence, degree of disability, loss of symptom control, or treatment-response finding was supposed to end “quite some time”?

It also raises the informational issue: if substantial benefit was anticipated, was there value in a carefully controlled trial to determine which symptoms—including the longstanding foot syndrome—were actually dopamine responsive?


6. 24 APRIL 2018 — “NO GREAT LOSS IN ‘NOT KNOWING’” THE GENETIC DIAGNOSIS

QUOTE:

“It doesn’t particularly change current management — so there is no great loss in ‘not knowing’ currently right now.”

INTERESTING PERSPECTIVE:

This statement concerned delaying PRKN/PARK2 genetic clarification despite both the treating neurologist and Prof Healy considering PARK2 a serious possibility.

The unusual feature is that elsewhere the neurologist himself says a genetic diagnosis might assist with:

“an understanding of Levodopa sensitivities.”

So the expert question is whether “does it immediately change the prescription?” was too narrow a test of the value of knowing.

A PRKN diagnosis might conceivably be relevant not only to the name of the disease, but to interpretation of the phenotype, dystonia, treatment responsiveness, dyskinesia susceptibility and surveillance.

The quote is therefore important because it reveals the criterion being used to value diagnostic information.


7. 28 FEBRUARY 2019 — “AS LONG AS IS HUMANLY POSSIBLE”

QUOTE:

“There will come a time or sooner or later Sinemet-levodopa will have to be looked at.”

followed by:

“...he is only 41 and therefore it is my preference to try to leave having to look at levodopa for as long as is humanly possible.”

INTERESTING PERSPECTIVE:

This is unusually strong language.

By 2019 the original cautious approach had become an explicit long-term management preference: not merely “use cautiously,” but postpone consideration for “as long as is humanly possible.”

This matters because a management preference expressed that strongly can potentially become an anchoring proposition.

The expert should therefore examine whether subsequent evidence was being used to test that policy, or predominantly being interpreted within it.

The wording is especially significant when read against the earlier acknowledgement that levodopa might produce major benefit and that delaying it might eventually be regretted.


8. 2019 — ACKNOWLEDGED LIMIT OF SPECIALIST EXPERTISE
   [Contained in the longitudinal neurology formulation reproduced in the 2019 material]

QUOTE:

“In reality, while I am not an expert on these conditions (I am not a movement disorders neurologist)…”

INTERESTING PERSPECTIVE:

There is nothing inherently problematic about a clinician acknowledging the limits of his expertise.

The reason the statement is significant is what surrounds it:

• unusually young Parkinsonism;
• atypical symmetry;
• dystonic features;
• suspected PRKN/PARK2;
• questions about levodopa sensitivity;
• genetic uncertainty; and
• an unusually consequential strategy regarding dopamine replacement.

The expert question is therefore not why the neurologist admitted his limits.

It is:

At what point did those acknowledged limits make continuing movement-disorders specialist involvement clinically important?


9. 27 OCTOBER 2021 — “SPARE SINEMET ... ALL OVER THE WORLD”

QUOTE:

“It is a general principle of Parkinson’s management all over the world that we try and spare Sinemet for as long as possible in people who are very young...”

followed by:

“Additionally when Sinemet is used, the aim should be to keep the dosing as absolutely minimal as possible.”

INTERESTING PERSPECTIVE:

This is striking because a patient-specific management strategy is expressed as a near-universal principle.

By this stage Sinemet had finally been introduced, partly because Kemadrin/procyclidine was being removed.

The important question is whether the governing therapeutic objective remained:

MINIMISE LEVODOPA

rather than:

OPTIMISE LEVODOPA EXPOSURE WITHIN THIS PARTICULAR PATIENT’S THERAPEUTIC WINDOW.

Those are not necessarily the same strategy.

Later evidence suggests this patient could experience both dyskinesia at higher dopaminergic states and disabling symptoms at lower states. That makes dose timing, interval, formulation and coverage potentially as important as total dose.


10. 27 OCTOBER 2021 — DBS “ONLY EVER” FOR TREMOR

QUOTE:

“He mentioned about DBS today. As I have reminded him, the DBS is only ever for the tremor element. It will not help with anything else.”

INTERESTING PERSPECTIVE:

This is probably the most obviously categorical clinical statement in the record.

The wording is not:

“DBS would not presently be appropriate for this patient.”

Nor is it:

“DBS may be particularly useful for tremor.”

It says that DBS is “only ever” for tremor and “will not help with anything else.”

That is a materially different proposition.

Its relevance to the wider case is that it provides the independent expert with a concrete example against which to assess the movement-disorders knowledge informing other complex treatment decisions at that time.


11. 27 OCTOBER 2021 — SINEMET EXPECTED TO “MORE THAN COMPENSATE” FOR KEMADRIN

QUOTE:

“[Patient] is obviously hoping that the Sinemet change with the increased dose now to 4 times per day will more than compensate for the Kemadrin removal...”

INTERESTING PERSPECTIVE:

This is important less because the wording is bizarre than because it exposes an untested therapeutic proposition.

Kemadrin had previously produced profound benefit and its withdrawal had generated documented foot-specific deterioration.

It was now being removed and Sinemet was expected to compensate.

The key longitudinal question is:

Was “more than compensate” ever tested against the feet themselves?

If the adequacy of substitution was judged predominantly from general Parkinsonian motor performance, an important symptom-specific outcome may have been left unresolved.


12. MARCH 2025 — SINEMET “SHOULD NOT DO ANYTHING FOR PAIN”

QUOTE:

“He knows very well that once the Sinemet hits his system that the feet feel better from a pain perspective.”

followed immediately by:

“Sinemet should not do anything for pain but it will relieve the experience of pain to at least some degree.”

INTERESTING PERSPECTIVE:

The juxtaposition is striking.

The clinician acknowledges a reproducible patient-specific observation:

SINEMET ENTERS SYSTEM → FOOT PAIN IMPROVES.

He then states that Sinemet “should not do anything for pain,” while immediately explaining that it nevertheless relieves the patient's experience of pain.

There may be a legitimate distinction between treating the underlying peripheral/structural pain generator and modulating the experience of pain through dopaminergic state.

But that distinction makes the observed treatment-state relationship MORE clinically interesting, not less.

The question becomes:

What did the reproducible relationship between levodopa state and foot pain tell the treating team about the architecture of the syndrome?


13. MARCH 2025 — “PAIN IS NOT REALLY A DIRECT PART OF PARKINSON’S AT ALL”

QUOTE:

“Quite simply, pain is not really a direct part of Parkinson’s at all. Certainly if person has dystonia a person can have painful dystonia.”

followed immediately by:

“But I have little doubt other than that if we increased the Sinemet, he would probably get some further relief from the pain.”

INTERESTING PERSPECTIVE:

This is internally awkward wording.

Within essentially the same paragraph the note says:

• pain is not directly part of Parkinson’s;
• dystonia can be painful;
• this patient has a dystonic disorder;
• Sinemet predictably relieves some of his pain.

The clinically useful question is not whether all of the pain was “Parkinson’s pain.”

It is whether the binary distinction between Parkinsonian and non-Parkinsonian pain was obscuring a mixed syndrome in which structural pain generation, dystonia, abnormal loading and dopaminergic modulation could coexist.


14. MARCH 2025 — THE OVERNIGHT GAP APPEARS ALMOST IN PLAIN SIGHT

QUOTE:

“His own feeling in this regard would be, that he feels that perhaps an element of this is how bad the Parkinson’s is expected to be in the morning times — because he has not had medications for example overnight.”

followed by:

“In this regard, there would be the option on giving him a slow release Sinemet at night time?”

INTERESTING PERSPECTIVE:

This is striking retrospectively because it identifies the therapeutic hypothesis almost exactly:

LONG OVERNIGHT DOPAMINERGIC GAP
→ SEVERE MORNING FOOT SYMPTOMS
→ TEST EXTENDED OVERNIGHT COVERAGE.

The subsequent controlled-release trial then reportedly produced profound relief.

This does not establish that the same intervention would have worked years earlier.

But it makes the earlier 2022 morning “warm-up” phenomenon particularly important and raises the question of when the overnight-coverage hypothesis first became reasonably testable.


15. 25 MARCH 2025 — “WE HAVE KNOWN”

QUOTE:

“In essence we have known that [Patient] has Parkinson’s; we have known it would be Sinemet or other dopamine agonist responsive...”

followed by:

“...it has been known and acknowledged that Sinemet helps his experience of ongoing pain.”

INTERESTING PERSPECTIVE:

This is one of the most important retrospective statements.

The problem is not necessarily that it is false in broad outline.

The issue is temporal precision.

The contemporaneous record shows:

• diagnostic uncertainty and atypicality;
• a specific PRKN/PARK2 hypothesis;
• predicted dopaminergic responsiveness;
• deliberate non-testing with levodopa initially;
• an unresolved foot relationship;
• and later development of explicit evidence that Sinemet affected pain.

The phrase “we have known” compresses prediction, expectation and later observation into a single continuous state of knowledge.

An expert should therefore distinguish:

WHAT WAS SUSPECTED
→ WHAT WAS PREDICTED
→ WHAT WAS ACTUALLY TESTED
→ WHEN IT BECAME KNOWN.


16. 25 MARCH 2025 — RETROSPECTIVE PREGABALIN “LEVODOPA-SPARING” RATIONALE

QUOTE:

“His Lyrica pregabalin treatment was introduced for the neuropathic pain, of whatever cause, in part to try to spare further Sinemet increase...”

INTERESTING PERSPECTIVE:

This is important because the contemporaneous initiation pathway records pregabalin being introduced by the GP for severe burning/neuropathic-type pain.

The later statement gives pregabalin an additional strategic role:

LEVODOPA-SPARING.

That may accurately describe what pregabalin subsequently became useful for.

But the expert should distinguish a later management function from the contemporaneously documented reason for starting the medication.

Otherwise later therapeutic logic risks being projected backwards into an earlier decision.


17. 25 MARCH 2025 — “PROFOUND RELIEF”

QUOTE:

“Now his Sinemet treatment has been recently escalated at clinic, with a plan from clinic to do this slowly and carefully as Sinemet CR, which has provided him with profound relief currently.”

INTERESTING PERSPECTIVE:

This is the opposite endpoint to the 2017 “we may regret” statement.

In 2017:
LEVODOPA MIGHT WORK VERY WELL → DELAY MAY LATER BE REGRETTED.

By 2025:
EXTENDED LEVODOPA COVERAGE → “PROFOUND RELIEF.”

That does not prove that CR should have been prescribed in 2017.

What it does is create the central retrospective expert question:

How much earlier patient-specific evidence should have caused the original management strategy to be re-tested?

The significance lies in the eight-year sequence between those two statements, not in either quotation standing alone.
